### 1. Non-Anglophone coverage of Sahel [sah-HEL] realignment

Start in the Sahel, because the English-language wire services have the shape of the story wrong. Or at least incomplete. Over the past ten days, GDELT [GEE-delt] machine-translated sources from Mali, Niger, Burkina Faso, and Senegal have been narrating a security pact shift in the region. The wire version is fairly clean: a military-led government has renegotiated its external security arrangements. Western outlets lead with the geopolitical frame—France loses another foothold, Russia or a Gulf state gains one, and the map tilts further away from the old Francophone order.

The local coverage is not organized around that headline. It is organized around the question “who benefits?”—and the answer is much less abstract. In Bamako, the story leads with the payment arrears owed to local militia units and whether the new partner will actually pay them in cash rather than equipment. In Niamey, the coverage spends more time on the mining concessions and fuel-supply contracts attached to the security deal than on the identity of the foreign advisors. One Senegalese outlet, translated by GDELT, frames the entire arrangement as a labor-export bargain: young men from the region are supposed to get construction and logistics jobs in the Gulf in exchange for basing rights and military support. That is not a defense story. That is a political-economy story.

The local framing also spends time on the domestic opposition figures who are quietly supporting the deal—not because they like the new foreign patron, but because it weakens a rival faction inside the security establishment. That detail does not appear in the wires. It matters because the pact is less a clean break with the West than a reordering of internal power. The outside observers are not the main characters; they are the new rent-seekers who happen to have arrived at the right time.

**Why this matters:** The local framing inverts the standard Western read—a reminder of how much the story depends on the desk.

### 2. Export controls as industrial policy, not just national security

Late last week, a new export-control rule for advanced AI accelerators appeared in the Federal Register. American outlets covered it the way they usually cover such things: the Pentagon, the National Security Council, the threat of Chinese military applications. The US-led frame is essentially “we are restricting a dangerous technology for national security reasons.” That is a real part of the story. But it is not the part that non-US readers saw.

In Taipei, Seoul, and Brussels, the rule is being read as industrial policy wearing a security uniform. The rule tightens licensing for a class of chips above a certain interconnect speed and memory bandwidth. It creates a two-tier system: some countries get broad access, others get none, and a middle group gets case-by-case review. The non-US coverage points out that the real effect is to herd the next wave of large-model training into jurisdictions the United States can influence. A Seoul daily, for example, argues that the target is not only the People’s Liberation Army but the emerging sovereign-AI stacks in the Gulf, Southeast Asia, and India. The worry is not that someone will build a bomb; it is that someone will train a frontier model without needing Nvidia [en-VID-ee-ah] chips from a US-approved cloud provider.

That distinction changes the stakes. If this is a security story, the relevant countermeasure is a spy agency or a treaty. If this is an industrial-policy story, the relevant countermeasure is a subsidy, a domestic chip program, or a preferential trade arrangement. The non-US coverage often includes numbers: how many gigawatts of new data-center capacity each country is planning, how many AI PhDs they expect to graduate, what share of their current compute already comes from the United States. Those are not the metrics of a Pentagon press conference. They are the metrics of competition for the next industrial revolution.

**Why this matters:** The local frame is about who gets to train the next wave of models, not about a press conference.

### 3. An essay on the exhaustion of the autofiction novel

The London Review of Books [L-R-B] has a long piece this week arguing that the autofiction [AWE-toe-FIK-shun] novel has calcified into mannerism. The author’s provocation is blunt: what began as a liberation from the big social novel has become a set of tics. The narrator who is also the author. The photograph described in real time. The parent’s illness or suicide. The cancelled event. The essay does not deny that some early autofiction was genuinely new. It changed the relationship between the writer and the page, between the reader and the claim of truth. But the LRB critic argues the mode has now hardened into a genre with its own conventions—and that those conventions are as predictable as the nineteenth-century marriage plot they once rebelled against.

The essay is worth reading for the way it separates two things that often get confused. The first is the autobiographical detail. The second is the formal risk. Autofiction, the piece argues, promised the second but now often delivers only the first. The writer reveals a private fact but takes no real formal risk. The reader is asked to be interested because the material is true, not because the sentence is doing something difficult. That creates a peculiar kind of exhaustion: the book feels intimate on the surface but is actually quite safe underneath. The “I” has become a brand, not a method.

The essay ends with a question rather than a prediction. If the autofictional mode is exhausted, what returns? The author suggests it may be plot—real plot, with other people in it, and consequences that are not organized around the narrator’s perception. Or it may be a different kind of first person, one willing to be boring, ordinary, or wrong. The piece does not name a successor. It just says the current mode has stopped being a question and become a costume.

**Why this matters:** A useful lens on why so much acclaimed new fiction feels the same—and what might come next.

## Paper of the Day

### Spacing effects, revisited for the AI-tutoring era

Today’s paper is a direct descendant of a famous 2008 study by Cepeda [seh-PEH-dah] and colleagues. The original question was simple: if you study something now, how long should you wait before reviewing it? The answer was not a fixed number. It was a ratio. The optimal gap between study and review scales with how long you want to remember. If you want to remember something for a week, review after about a day. If you want to remember it for a year, review after about a month. That finding became one of the most durable results in learning science.

The new paper asks whether that rule survives contact with the AI-tutoring era. It is not a traditional classroom study. It is a large randomized trial embedded in a mobile review app, with thousands of adult learners. The app schedules review sessions automatically. Some learners got textbook spacing—short gaps, frequent reviews, the kind of thing that feels good and produces quick gains. Others got a spacing algorithm derived from Cepeda’s ratio. The app waited longer before re-showing the material, sometimes so long that the learner felt slightly embarrassed when the card appeared again. Then the researchers measured retention weeks later, not at the end of the session.

The headline result is a replication, but with a twist. The optimal spacing ratio held. The best performance came when the gap between study and review was roughly ten to twenty percent of the retention interval. If you wanted to remember for one month, you reviewed after about four to six days. If you wanted to remember for six months, you reviewed after about three to five weeks. The app-based setting did not weaken the original finding. If anything, it made the ratio more precise, because the app could measure the exact time between exposures.

But the new paper found something else. When the app deliberately made the review slightly harder—by delaying it a bit beyond the easy window, or by mixing old material with new—the learners rated the session as worse. They felt less fluent. They made more errors. But their retention one month later was better. The sessions that felt worse were the sessions that worked. The authors call this the “app-induced desirable difficulty.” The algorithm was not just scheduling practice; it was choosing the level of difficulty that forced the brain to reconstruct the memory instead of recognizing it.

**Why this matters:** The optimal gap scales with how long you want to remember—a directly usable rule, not folklore.

The practical takeaway is this: decide how long you want to hold something. If it is a week, review tomorrow. If it is a month, review in four to six days. If it is a year, review in about six weeks, then again at roughly double that. The app data suggest that you should tolerate the unpleasant feeling of a review that is just slightly too hard. That feeling is not a bug. It is the mechanism doing its work.

## One Idea

### Desirable difficulties, applied

This is a learning-science note, but it is really a note about how to think. The term comes from Robert Bjork [byork], a psychologist at UCLA. The basic insight is that the difficulty of retrieving a memory is not a side effect of learning. It is the learning. The brain is not a tape recorder. It is a retrieval machine. Every time you pull a memory out, you strengthen the pathway and weaken competing associations. If the retrieval is too easy, you have not practiced the hard part. You have just re-read the prompt.

Rereading feels good because the text supplies all the cues. The sentences flow. The concepts seem familiar. That familiarity is a trap. It produces what learning scientists call “fluent, but not durable.” You think you know the material because you recognize it. But recognition is not recall. Recognition requires a cue. Recall requires you to generate the answer from partial or missing cues. That generative act is where the durable memory is built. The same logic applies to highlighting, to watching a lecture twice, to nodding along in a meeting. All of them feel like learning. Most of them are not.

A desirable difficulty is a task that makes retrieval harder but still possible. You struggle, and then you succeed. That success, after effort, produces a stronger memory than an easy success. The key is that the struggle must be real but winnable. If you fail too often, the difficulty is no longer desirable. It is just noise. The sweet spot is the place where you have to work, but you can still get there.

Now, how to use this. In a conversation, do not just listen and nod. After someone explains a new idea, say it back in your own words. Ask a question that forces you to apply it: “So would that also mean…?” The awkward pause while you search for the right formulation is the thing that makes the idea yours. If you cannot say it back, you did not understand it yet. That is useful information, not a social failure.

In your own research, adopt a ninety-second rule. After reading a paper or a report, close the tab and write down the central argument from memory before you open your notes. Then compare. The act of reconstructing the argument—even badly—will teach you more than any highlighter. Do the same after a seminar or a meeting. The ninety seconds of self-testing after the event will often be worth more than the event itself, because they force you to find the structure instead of just experiencing the flow.

In studying, use flashcards, but delay the answer. Do not flip the card immediately. Give yourself ten or fifteen seconds to generate the answer first. Mix problem types rather than practicing one kind at a time. Change the room you study in. All of these introduce small, productive frictions. They make the session feel less smooth. That is the point. The smoothness was the illusion of learning.

There is a deeper version of this idea. The feeling of ease is not just a trap in the classroom. It is a trap in thinking more generally. When an argument is too familiar, we assume it is correct. When a story is too fluent, we assume it is true. One way to break that is to introduce a desirable difficulty into your own reasoning: translate the claim into your own terms, give a counterexample, or explain it to a critical friend. If you cannot reconstruct the argument without the original words, you may not know it as well as you think.

**Why this matters:** The feeling of ease is a trap: the work that feels worse in the moment is often the work that sticks.
