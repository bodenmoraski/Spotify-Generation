## The World

### 1. The Arctic reopens as a geopolitical stage

International outlets picked up a phrase this week that would have sounded like a Cold War throwback a decade ago: the Arctic as a geopolitical theatre. The physical facts are not new. Sea ice has thinned enough that northern shipping lanes are opening. Seabed resources are becoming economically legible. Russia, China, the United States, and Nordic states are all updating their Arctic strategies. But the headline marks a shift in framing: the region has moved from a climate story to a competition story. What often gets lost is the local layer. Indigenous northern communities and small Arctic states have operated in this territory for centuries. They are not bystanders being discovered; they are negotiators with sudden strategic leverage. The dangerous gap is between fast-moving security language and the slower legal machinery of the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea. You can map overlapping claims. You cannot map who will enforce them, or whether enforcement will be legal or just operational. Insurance underwriters are already recalculating hull risk for Arctic transit, which is less dramatic than a navy but more likely to decide whether the route gets used at all. Note the phrase “geopolitical theatre” itself: it invites analysts to treat the region as a stage rather than a home.

**Why this matters:** The Arctic is becoming a place where security logic races ahead of law, and the people who live there may have the most to lose.

### 2. Mechanization was absorbed by the young, not by retraining

A new working paper in economic history, flagged by Tyler Cowen [COW-en], feels like a direct reply to our automation anxiety. The study uses 170 million full-count British census observations between 1851 and 1911 to examine English bootmaking as it mechanized. The familiar story says machines arrive, artisans get displaced, and the labor market hollows out. The census data tell a different story. Roughly 153,000 artisanal jobs disappeared as skills became obsolete, while around 140,000 new specialized jobs appeared. But incumbent artisans did not take the new jobs, and they were not pushed into unemployment. Entry collapsed. Young men simply stopped entering the old trade. The new jobs went primarily to young workers, often in different locations. The adjustment was absorbed by cohorts, not by retraining. The policy lesson is uncomfortable: if the mechanism is entry collapse, then wage subsidies for incumbent workers may not help much. What helps is lowering the cost of mobility for the young: housing, training in new places, information about where the new jobs actually are. If the same dynamic holds today, then the people most affected by AI automation may not be experienced professionals. They may be graduates deciding whether to enter shrinking fields at all.

**Why this matters:** If mechanization mostly redirected young entrants rather than displacing experienced workers, then our retraining-heavy policy imagination may be missing the generational and geographic pain.

### 3. A queer Afghan novel reframes the War on Terror

On the twenty-fifth anniversary of September 11, Literary Hub has run an essay that tries to wrestle the War on Terror away from Washington retrospectives and put it back into the bodies of people who lived it. Steven W. Thrasher reads two books together: Bobuq Sayed’s [BOO-book SIGH-yed] debut novel _No God But Us_, and José Esteban Muñoz’s [ho-ZAY es-TEH-bahn MOON-yoze] _The Sense of Brown_. The novel follows two queer Afghan refugees whose lives are upended by the American war. Thrasher’s argument is that too much of the anniversary coverage is organized by American loss and American strategy. The Costs of War project at Brown University puts the death toll at 4.5 million, a number that would be impossible to fit into a story about precision strikes and democratic transition. Reading through queer refugee fiction changes the moral geometry: the protagonist is not a soldier or a planner, survival itself is the plot. Thrasher is pointed about the asymmetry: American readers receive the war as a series of policy choices; these characters receive it as weather, infrastructure, and fear.

**Why this matters:** The war is not something to commemorate and close; it is still being lived by people whose stories rarely make the retrospective panels.

### 4. K-pop is doing quiet diplomatic work for Seoul

From the Brazilian press, a piece whose headline runs, “From K-pop to diplomacy: how culture drives the Brazil–South Korea partnership.” It would be easy to file this under soft power and ignore it. The deeper argument is that Korean cultural exports have become a formal part of the diplomatic relationship. Trade missions now open with music. Brazilian universities run Korean-language programs. Korean food franchises are expanding in São Paulo [sah-oo PAH-loo]. For South Korea, this is not just popularity; it is de-risking. It creates domestic constituencies in countries that might otherwise see Korea as only a semiconductor supplier or a geopolitical ally of convenience. For Brazil, the relationship offers diversification away from traditional partners. But the exchange is not symmetrical. Korea exports far more cultural content than it imports, and the diplomatic dividend accrues mostly to Seoul. The piece frames the relationship as mutual, but the structure is one-directional. What makes this more than a lifestyle story is the timing: Brazil has been trying to build points of influence outside the usual North Atlantic axis, and Seoul has been trying to make its export model less dependent on any single customer.

**Why this matters:** Soft power is not a concert; it is infrastructure that makes certain alliances feel natural before any treaty is signed.

### 5. Perplexity hands a frontier model the production keys

OpenAI announced this week that Perplexity is using GPT-6 Astra for end-to-end operations: writing communications, changing software, and monitoring production systems, with much less frequent human checks. A vendor case study is usually the part of the news you skip. But the word “end-to-end” deserves attention. It means the model is no longer a co-pilot that drafts a response and waits for approval. It has authority to act directly across systems. The blog post emphasizes accuracy improvements. The real signal is that a consumer AI company is comfortable letting a frontier model touch production. That tells you how far deployment has moved beyond the lab safety framework. The less frequent check-ins are not a detail; they are the thing that turns a language model into an operator. The risk story is not that the model fails a benchmark. It is that the model fails in a way no one catches because the dashboard no longer requires a human to look. The deployment also raises an unanswered question: when the company says the model checks in less frequently, who is responsible for the errors between check-ins? The labor is being withdrawn before the accountability is assigned.

**Why this matters:** Reducing the frequency of human check-ins is a safety decision, even when it is written as a customer success story.

## Paper of the Day

### Data Scarcity and Model Sparsity: Mixtures-of-Experts Overfit More to Repeated Data

Today’s paper asks an uncomfortable question about the models everyone is building. The easy human text is running out. Labs now repeat data during training. For dense language models, researchers have studied what repetition does: a little is fine, too much and the model starts to memorize. But the most efficient large models are now sparse. They are called mixture-of-experts, or MoE [emm-oh-ee], models: only a fraction of the total parameters activate on any input. The paper from Atindra Jha [ah-TIN-drah jah], Margaret Li, Jure Leskovec [YOO-reh LESS-koh-veck], Percy Liang, and Luke Zettlemoyer asks: what happens to MoE models when the data gets repeated?

The researchers varied repetition rates across single-domain and multi-domain mixes. They tested models from 80 million active parameters to 1 billion active parameters, with up to 8.5 billion total parameters. The result was surprisingly clean. MoE models degrade much faster under data repetition than dense models. Dense models could repeat the data eight times with minimal damage. MoE models began to suffer at four repeats. By thirty-two repeats, the sparse models had lost their advantage and underperformed dense models. The effect increased with sparsity, and it was governed by total parameter count, not just the active parameters. That is a crucial detail: the unused capacity is still visible to overfitting.

The paper tried existing regularization methods as a remedy. Some worked. Dropout helped, and strong masking-based regularization brought back much of the lost performance. The so-what is this: compute-efficient sparsity and data-efficient training may be in tension. If your training set is repeated, the sparse model you chose for efficiency may be the one that overfits. The authors do not say to abandon MoEs. They show the failure mode and point toward regularization. But the result should change how labs talk about data scarcity. The usual story is that sparsity helps because you can scale total parameters without scaling compute. This paper says that benefit is partly a loan, and repetition is the interest payment.

**Why this matters:** As the next generation of models is trained on synthetic and repeated data, the architecture that looked cheapest may be the one that fails when data gets recycled.

## One Idea

### The fiber between the stones

Here is a mental model for your week, and it comes from a piece in Nautilus about Stone Age technology. Benjamin Taub writes that the Stone Age is misnamed. We call it the Stone Age because stone survives. But the most important technologies of early human life were probably made of fiber: rope, twine, and thread. They rotted. The archaeological record is not a representative sample; it is a durability filter. What remains becomes the story.

But fiber was the connective tissue of daily life. It lashed stone axe heads to handles. It made nets, snares, fishing lines, bags, clothing, baskets, roofs, and boats. Without string, you cannot carry food back to the group. Without knots, you cannot bind the blade. Without thread, you cannot make clothes for cold climates. So when we picture a prehistoric person holding a stone tool, we are seeing only the part of the tool that did not rot. The larger system was fiber.

This is not just ancient history. The same filter operates on anything you study. In organizations, the org chart is stone; the informal workflows that actually get things done are fiber. In science, the published paper is stone; the failed experiments, lab conversations, and data-cleaning decisions are fiber. In AI, the benchmark is stone; the prompt engineering, data curation, and model babysitting are fiber. The perishable layer is often where the real work happens, but it disappears from the record.

So here is the question you can take into your own research: what rotted? Before you trust a dataset, an archive, or an official history, ask what could not have been preserved. The answer will not be in the index. It will be in the gaps.

A second example, from the Public Domain Review, reinforces the point. Early nineteenth-century flap books taught children metamorphosis by letting them physically manipulate paper flaps: Adam into Eve into a mermaid, a lion into a griffin into an eagle, all while learning about mortality. The knowledge was not just on the page; it was in the moving part. The medium itself was perishable and manipulable. These books survived because some caregivers kept them, but their lessons depended on a child’s hand. The durable text was only half the object.

Use this in conversation. When someone says “the evidence clearly shows X,” pause and ask: “What could not survive to become evidence?” Use it in self-improvement. The visible outputs of your week are stone; the sleep, maintenance, and small conversations that made them possible are fiber. Do not mistake the surviving artifacts for the whole system.

**Why this matters:** The feeling of understanding history, an organization, or even yourself is often based on the stones. The fiber may tell a different story.
