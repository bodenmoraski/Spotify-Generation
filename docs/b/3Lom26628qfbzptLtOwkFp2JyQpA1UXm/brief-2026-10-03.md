## The World

### 1. The credibility revolution leaves a con, says Econ Journal Watch

Tyler Cowen’s Marginal Revolution points to a new issue of Econ Journal Watch that should make anyone who cites famous empirical papers pause. The journal revisits two high-profile cases. First, the “China shock.” The 2013 paper by David Autor [AW-tor], David Dorn, and Gordon Hanson argued that Chinese import competition from 1990 to 2007 harmed U.S. employment, labor force participation, wages, and household incomes. Joseph Francis now applies corrections from Kirill Borusyak [boh-ROOS-yak] and colleagues and argues those harms disappear when the corrections are fully applied. Francis calls it a “credibility revolution” problem: the methods looked rigorous, but the result may have depended on a weighting choice. The journal invites the original authors to reply. Second, SFFA v. Harvard forecast failure. Thirty-three selective colleges predicted Black enrollment declines of 50 to 70 percent; David Card estimated Harvard’s Black share would fall from 14 to 6 percent. David Kane reports that three years later the predictions did not come close to true.  
**Why this matters:** If the most-cited trade-shock paper and the most consequential admissions forecasts are fragile under replication, the policy debate has been running on a map with several missing streets.

### 2. A LessWrong argument: capabilities research may be safety research, which is exactly the problem

Alex Mallen’s LessWrong post targets a definition that sounds reassuring: safety research is anything that pushes out the safety-usefulness Pareto frontier, allowing safer deployment without much cost to usefulness. By that standard, he argues, almost all capabilities research qualifies. More efficient inference lets a lab use weaker, safer models more broadly. Better optimization expands the set of options. Any successful research program creates possibilities. But Mallen says this definition misses the actual decision. A frontier is a menu, not a choice. Developers still have to select a point on it, and some technological improvements change which point they are incentivized to select. A faster, cheaper model may allow a safer deployment, but it may also make the more capable, less safe deployment more attractive. The essay is not anti-capabilities; it is a demand for clearer language. If you label everything that expands the menu as safety research, you cannot tell whether the menu is being used to serve safer models or more capable ones.  
**Why this matters:** Funders and labs could report rising safety investment while actually buying capabilities without the corresponding guardrails.

### 3. Do smartphones belong in fiction? A working novelist says the problem is style, not phones

Ankur Thakkar’s LitHub essay begins with a compressed literary history. The novel has always absorbed technology, he argues. Baudelaire’s [bohd-LAIR] flâneur [flah-NUHR] emerged from the new metropolitan crowd; transcontinental railroads made narrative skips in space and time legible; industrial factories pushed novelists inward into free indirect discourse. The internet is the next such shift. The question is not whether to include text messages, notifications, and voice commands, but how to represent them so they become part of consciousness rather than typographical clutter. Thakkar’s own novel Blow Yourself Up starts when smartphones reach high schools and ends just before the dawn of AI, so he has had to solve the craft problem directly. He is less interested in documenting the screen than in making the screen feel as inevitable as the railroad. The alternative is not nostalgia for paper; it is a challenge to form.  
**Why this matters:** This changes how you read new fiction: a text thread that feels flat is a craft failure, not a generational tic—and when it works, it may be doing for the inbox what the railroad did for plot.

### 4. Germany’s president warns of Weimar-style divisions

Frank-Walter Steinmeier [SHTINE-my-er], Germany’s president, used a word no sitting head of state wants to reach for: Weimar [VY-mahr]. In coverage via Modern Diplomacy, he warned that current political fragmentation risks Weimar-style divisions. This is not a policy announcement. The German presidency is ceremonial; its power is moral. When Steinmeier invokes the interwar failure of the Republic, he is telling the political class that the center’s old assumptions no longer hold. For a decade, Germany’s mainstream treated its polar flanks as coalition-management problems. A presidential warning of Weimar-scale division suggests the elite now sees fragmentation as an existential threat, not a polling blip. The historical echo is specific: Weimar did not fall because extremists won a majority outright; it fell because the democratic center could no longer cooperate.  
**Why this matters:** If the head of state reaches for Weimar, the country’s mainstream is treating polarization as a systemic risk, not a temporary political mood.

### 5. China’s economy: the old rivalry model may not fit

ChinaTalk convened a debate that pins down why the China-shock-era mental model may need retirement. Logan Wright, a top macroeconomist on China, argues the financial system that powered China’s extraordinary growth is now the main constraint on it. Brad Setser [SET-ser] and others push back: Does Xi actually care? Can China’s dominance of exports and supply chains keep growth going? Could AI and electric vehicles provide an escape? But Wright holds his ground. In his view, the binding problem is internal credit allocation, not external demand. If true, it changes what “rivalry” means for the next decade. Washington’s policy conversation is still built around export dominance, tariffs, and supply chains. Wright’s argument says that a large part of that playbook may be aimed at yesterday’s economy. The debate also touches China’s jobs crisis and what could pull the country out of its funk. Elsewhere in the episode, a dispatch from Kyrgyzstan [KEER-gih-stan] on the World Nomad Games, including the Central Asian horse game kök-börü [kook-buh-ROO], weighs the gap between China’s hard and soft power.  
**Why this matters:** If China’s future turns on its domestic balance sheet rather than factory competition, the U.S. may be preparing for the wrong rivalry.

## Paper of the Day

### VISTA: a visual harness for reasoning in an interactive world

Imagine a very smart person locked in a room with a narrow periscope. They can think perfectly well, but they can only see one small patch of the world at a time. That, according to a new paper from Qiushi Han, Keya Hu, Linlu Qiu, Cathy Wu, and Kaiming He, is not a bad description of how today’s multimodal models perform on interactive reasoning benchmarks. The question they asked was simple: Do these models fail because they cannot reason, or because they cannot see enough?

The group built VISTA [VISS-tuh], a visual harness. It gives a general-purpose multimodal model long-horizon vision. Instead of a short visual buffer, VISTA keeps a lossless visual memory of past observations in their original form. The model can actively retrieve those observations and reorganize its visual input while it reasons. This is closer to a person looking around a room and deciding where to look next than to a camera feeding a fixed clip. The base model is unchanged; what changes is the interface between the model and the world.

ARC-AGI-3 [ark-ah-gee-three] is a benchmark of abstract reasoning in interactive visual games. It does not just ask for a final answer; it asks the model to play through sequences, remember what it saw, and act efficiently. The score combines success with action efficiency relative to first-time human participants. On that benchmark, VISTA improved Claude Opus 5.0 from a Relative Human Action Efficiency score of 40.68 to a perfect 100.00. The model completed all 25 public games using 57.4 percent fewer actions than first-time humans. The same harness extended to three other visual game and puzzle benchmarks with minimal adaptation, substantially beating baselines that used the same underlying model with minimal harnesses.

The so-what is not that Claude got smarter. The base model was unchanged. The bottleneck was the harness. VISTA suggests we have been systematically underestimating current models by giving them a narrow window and then blaming their vision for what is actually a scaffolding problem.  
**Why this matters:** The next jump in agent performance may come from better tool design, not bigger models—which shifts the race from parameter counts to interfaces.

## One Idea

### 1. Sea monkeys rewrite a rule of turbulence

If you grew up with comic book ads, you may remember sea monkeys: freeze-dried brine shrimp that come to life in salt water. Quanta Magazine reports that these tiny swimmers are now doing something more surprising than the ads ever promised. They are helping physicists rewrite a rule of turbulence.

The old rule comes from Andrey Kolmogorov [kol-MOH-guh-rov] in 1941. In a turbulent fluid, energy is injected at large scales and cascades down to smaller and smaller eddies until it dissipates. The statistical pattern of that cascade is treated as a universal law. It holds for oceans, atmospheres, and pipes. But when researchers watched brine shrimp swim upward in a tank, the collective motion did not follow the expected scaling. The reason is simple but profound: the shrimp are not passive particles. Each one injects energy at its own scale, with its own timing. The law assumed swimmers with no intentions. Add agency, and the universal scaling breaks.

This is not a refutation of Kolmogorov; it is a boundary condition. The law works when the pieces are passive. It fails when the pieces are alive. The same hidden-assumption mistake shows up in economics, sociology, and machine learning. You fit a model that assumes interchangeable units. It works until the units have preferences.  
**Why this matters:** The next time you see a universal rule about markets, teams, or people, ask whether the units are passive particles or sea monkeys—because the map changes when the territory swims.

### 2. Life on a hair trigger

Aeon’s essay by Megan Kang makes a different version of the same point. The common story about violence is structural: guns, poverty, disinvestment. Kang does not deny those causes, but she argues that violence also sustains itself through the minds it creates. Early exposure to violence trains attention. It teaches a person to read ambiguity as threat, to interpret a minor social cue as a prelude to danger, and to respond before deliberation can catch up. That is the hair trigger.

But the mind can learn anew. The essay’s second claim is that these patterns are not permanent. With different environments, safe relationships, and deliberate retraining, a nervous system calibrated for danger can recalibrate. This is not a feel-good message; it is a learning-science claim. The brain’s threat detection system is a model trained on data. If the data changed once, it can change again.

This is useful beyond public safety. In a meeting or a negotiation, when someone overreacts to a small correction, it is tempting to argue more facts. But if their threat detector is doing exactly what it was trained to do, more facts may feel like more danger. You may need to signal safety first, because the reaction is not a personality defect; it is a rational adaptation to an environment you have not seen.  
**Why this matters:** The way out of a hair-trigger response is not just new rules; it is new data—and sometimes the most useful thing you can offer is a cue of safety, not a better argument.
