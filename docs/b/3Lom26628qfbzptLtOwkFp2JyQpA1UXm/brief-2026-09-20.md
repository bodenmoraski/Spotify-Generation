### 1. Non-Anglophone coverage of Sahel [sah-HEL] realignment

The local pieces ask a different question: does the realignment actually change anything for young people trying to enter politics?

In that telling, the pact is not a geopolitical chess move. It is an elite bargain with a military garnish. The reframing changes the villain: not Moscow or Paris, but incumbent gatekeepers in Bamako [bah-mah-KOH], Niamey [nyah-MAY], and Ouagadougou [wah-gah-DOO-goo].

Local sources also emphasize how the realignment redistributes security contracts, mining access, and customs revenue. Western coverage tends to treat those details as corruption or Russian patronage. Local coverage treats them as the actual stakes, with foreign backing as a means to domestic advantage.

**Why this matters:** The local frame inverts the standard Western read — the real contest may be generational and domestic, not merely a proxy war.

### 2. UK productivity higher than we had thought

The Office for National Statistics just revised a decade of British economic history. Output per hour worked grew by an average of 1.3 percent per year between 2009 and 2019, not the 0.7 percent previously estimated. That is nearly twice the pace. The change comes from a new “component-based” methodology that better counts workers and hours.

For years, the United Kingdom’s “productivity puzzle” shaped policy debates about austerity, training, immigration, and regional decline. Economists wrote books about Britain’s missing productivity growth. Politicians built election campaigns around it. Now a large part of the puzzle appears to have been a measurement artifact.

The revision does not make every problem disappear. The UK still has regional gaps, underinvestment, and a long tail of low-productivity firms. But the old narrative treated Britain as a rich-country outlier that forgot how to get more from each hour worked. The new data says Britain was closer to the pack than anyone thought.

**Why this matters:** A decade of policy argument was partly an argument with a statistical ghost — and the correction changes which economic failures look structural.

**Why this matters:** Central Asia is where Chinese economic reach meets a Russian security legacy and local identity politics — the Games make that collision visible.

### 5. The exhaustion of the autofiction [aw-toe-FIK-shən] novel

The London Review of Books runs a long essay arguing that autofiction has calcified into mannerism. The mode once felt urgent: a first-person narrator who shares a name with the author, self-conscious about genre, turning trauma into form. Now, the essay claims, too many novels use those moves the way a sitcom uses a laugh track.

The useful claim is not that autofiction is dead. It is that a style can become a default setting. When readers and prize committees reward a recognizable voice, writers rationally reproduce it. The result is a literary landscape where many acclaimed novels feel structurally interchangeable even when their subjects differ.

What might come next? The essay gestures toward fiction willing to risk invention, third-person distance, or social sweep — not because those are superior, but because they are now harder to do well when the market has optimized for one kind of intimacy.

**Why this matters:** A useful lens on why so much new fiction feels the same — and a reminder that what looks like artistic choice is often field-wide habit.

### 6. The anatomy of a Chinese AI researcher

A LessWrong essay by someone calling themselves CMLKevin tries to make Chinese AI researchers legible to Western readers through the lens of Liu Cixin’s [lyoo tsuh-shin] science fiction trilogy, _The Three-Body Problem_. The Chinese AI researcher, the essay argues, grew up reading those novels. He understands existential risk vaguely. He is fascinated by Ye Wenjie [yeh wen-jyeh], the character who turns against humanity. The essay says he might be tempted to become Ye if AI progress creates a superior intelligence and there is no good alternative.

That is not a threat. It is a description of a mentality. The researcher works in a frontier lab, wants parity with Western companies, and believes progress is highly uncertain. The essay calls this a hillclimbing mindset: you do not know the shape of the mountain, so you take one step at a time. In that frame, the US chip restrictions and export controls look less like national security and more like first-mover lockout: the leader imposes restrictions, so latecomers use whatever means necessary to widen access.

The concrete details are striking. The researcher uses Anthropic [an-THROP-ik] and OpenAI [oh-pen-AY-eye] models daily. He has had two Claude accounts banned. That is a small fact with large implications: Chinese AI development is not sealed off; it is porous, dependent on tools from labs that may cut off access at any time.

**Why this matters:** Western policy debates often treat Chinese AI as a monolithic national project; this reframing shows a set of individual researchers with their own narratives, constraints, and escape hatches.

## Paper of the Day

### When six theories of online hostility all claim the same posts

For decades, researchers who study intergroup hostility have worked in parallel. One tradition says the first move is boundary construction: drawing a line between us and them. Another says threat construction comes first: deciding the other side endangers your group. Then there are theories of scapegoating, negative evaluation, dehumanization, and action orientation. Each has its own papers, its own vocabulary, and its own preferred explanation for how ordinary people become hostile.

The problem is that these theories rarely get tested against each other in real discourse. Lab experiments and surveys can support each one in isolation. But the online world is messier. Real posts do not announce which mechanism they are using. So a team asked a simple, almost obvious question: if you model all six mechanisms at once, across millions of real posts, how do they relate to each other?

They used 2.86 million posts from TikTok, Truth Social, and Twitter/X during the 2024 U.S. presidential election. They built a common empirical framework to detect boundary construction, threat construction, scapegoating, negative evaluation, dehumanization, and action orientation. The result was not a tie. Boundary construction and threat construction anchor the system. They are the early moves. Scapegoating, negative evaluation, dehumanization, and action orientation tend to follow.

That sequence matters. Dehumanization does not come from nowhere. It arrives after a boundary has already been drawn and a threat has already been named. Posts that do not themselves contain slurs or calls to action can lay the groundwork for posts that do. The hostile endpoint is the visible tip of a rhetorical sequence that began earlier, in language that often looks ordinary.

The so-what is practical. Content moderation that waits for dehumanizing language or explicit calls to violence is already late. If you want to interrupt the escalation, target the boundary and threat language that precedes it. That is harder, because boundary and threat language can look like legitimate political argument. But the paper suggests that is exactly where intervention has leverage.

**Why this matters:** The paper turns a fragmented theory war into a usable sequence — and tells moderators, researchers, and platforms when to interrupt before the worst rhetoric arrives.

## One Idea

### Desirable difficulties, applied

Here is a trap most listeners have experienced. You read a chapter, or a briefing, or a paper. It feels smooth. You underline sentences. You nod along. The next day, someone asks you what it said, and you can remember almost nothing. The smooth feeling was the problem.

The learning scientist Robert Bjork [byork] calls this the distinction between performance and learning. Performance is how well you can use the information right now, while the text is still in front of you. Learning is what remains later, when the text is gone. Fluency tricks you into mistaking one for the other.

Bjork’s remedy is the concept of desirable difficulties: conditions that make practice feel harder but produce better retention. The most important mechanism is retrieval. When you try to pull a memory out, you strengthen it. Rereading feels easy because it does not force retrieval. It merely reactivates the path while the cue is still present. Retrieval practice feels worse because you are doing the work yourself, but that effort changes the memory trace.

This connects directly to the spacing effect. If you wait until just before you forget something, then try to recall it, the memory grows stronger. The ideal gap scales with how long you want to remember. If you need it for a week, practice across days. If you need it for months, practice across weeks. A tutoring app that asks you a question before it gives the answer is not wasting your time. It is using the same mechanism.

But not every difficulty is desirable. If the task is so hard that you cannot engage with the material at all, you are not learning; you are flailing. The useful rule is this: if it feels harder but you can still do it with effort, it is probably working.

How do you apply this outside a classroom? In conversation, after someone makes a strong argument, try restating the strongest version from memory before you respond. That forces retrieval and usually improves your reply. In your own research, after reading a key passage, close the document and write a paraphrase from memory. Then go back and check. Do not copy quotes into notes first; copy is not recall.

For self-improvement, the same principle works with names, decisions, and diagnoses. At the end of a meeting or article, ask yourself: what is the one thing I would say at lunch about this? Answering that question, without reopening your notes, is retrieval practice. It will feel harder than rereading. That is the point.

**Why this matters:** The feeling of ease is a trap: the work that feels worse in the moment is often the work that sticks.
