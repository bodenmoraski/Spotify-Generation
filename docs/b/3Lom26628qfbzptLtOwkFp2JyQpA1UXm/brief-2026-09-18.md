## The World

### 1. Pacing the frontier as a coordination problem, not a policy slogan

A new framework from LessWrong, by Raymond Douglas, Charles Dillon, Nikola Moore, Gavin Leech, and others, argues that “pacing AI” has become too narrow. Most people use it to mean delaying or pausing the most dangerous models in a race. Their paper, “Pacing the Frontier,” instead treats pacing as a broad class of interventions: anything that moderates the speed of AI development, deployment, or diffusion.

The uncomfortable claim is that haphazard pacing is already widespread. Labs delay releases for safety testing. Governments pause models after shocks. Export controls restrict compute. But these are isolated, unilateral actions that address only a small fraction of the problem—and the authors argue they can easily backfire. A pause in one lab can hand advantage to a less careful actor. Export controls can push demand toward unregulated supply chains.

The paper’s core point is that precedents are being set whether anyone intends them or not. The way pacing happens now will shape what later coordination looks like. The authors are pushing for a research agenda that treats pacing as something to be designed, not improvised after each crisis.

**Why this matters:** If pacing remains an ad hoc patchwork, the next AI shock will be governed by whoever acts first, not by a workable playbook.

### 2. The Uninhabitable Front: war as slow ecological destruction

Public Books has published an essay by Morgan Barry that reframes the cost of war not as immediate violence, but as the slow destruction of land. The piece opens with Israeli military aircraft flying low over the Lebanese villages of Ras Naqoura [rahs nah-KOO-rah] and Aita al-Shaab [AY-tah ahl-SHAHB], spraying a fine mist over agricultural fields. Within days, green fields, orchards, and olive groves turned brown. Farmers who had worked the same soil for generations watched their trees die.

The argument is that this is not collateral damage in a conventional sense. It is a form of environmental warfare that makes return impossible long after the shooting stops. The front line shifts from a place on a map to the soil itself.

Barry’s essay sits inside a larger emerging literature that treats ecological degradation as a military tactic: making territory uninhabitable, breaking the relation between people and land, and producing displacement that looks voluntary on paper because the fields no longer sustain life.

**Why this matters:** Environmental destruction usually outlasts ceasefires—and it determines who can return, or whether there is anything left to return to.

### 3. Susan Neiman on why the wrong evils get the attention

Susan Neiman [NYE-man] has a new piece in Literary Hub that begins with a story. A British podcast production company asked her about a project involving “modern manifestations of the concept of evil.” They had read her book. Then they asked if she had heard about a case of cannibalism in Florida. Her face fell.

Neiman’s argument is that lurid, rare cases—serial murder, cannibalism, true-crime horror—distract from the evils that are more common and far more dangerous. She quotes Adam Smith: the tendency to admire and almost worship the rich and powerful is the great and most universal cause of our moral sentiments. In her telling, that admiration is what lets ordinary systemic harm pass without the name “evil.”

The examples she gives are not cinematic. Making war to stay in power. Cutting aid that will cost poor people their lives. Those are not tabloid crimes, but they are easier to ignore precisely because they happen through institutions, budgets, and policy.

**Why this matters:** If your concept of evil only activates for rare monsters, you miss the ordinary decisions that do vastly more damage.

### 4. Trump’s AI deregulation stance is becoming a political liability

Transformer News has a sharp piece by Shakeel Hashim on the politics of AI deregulation. President Trump recently posted in all caps about a “SICK conspiracy against AI and Data Centers,” saying only China benefits from slowdowns, and adding: “WHOEVER WINS AI, WINS!” That came after leaders of major AI companies called for urgent federal legislation and a slower pace.

Hashim’s argument is that this is not just bad policy—it is bad politics. Polling consistently shows that Americans are extremely worried about AI. Sixty-three percent think there is at least a moderate risk that AI will destroy humanity. A pro-deregulation, race-China posture may energize a small base, but it puts the president on the wrong side of a broad public fear.

The piece also notes the strange alliances forming: security researchers used Claude to hack into OpenAI, while Sam Altman and Jensen Huang [JEN-sen hwahng] attend a Trump-Xi state dinner. The policy debate is no longer a niche technical fight.

**Why this matters:** AI is becoming a live electoral issue, and the deregulation position may be as politically risky as the technology itself.

### 5. The quiet power of papal diplomacy

GIS Reports has a piece examining the Vatican’s distinctive role in international affairs. The argument is that papal diplomacy works through a combination of moral authority, discretion, and access that states often lack. The Holy See does not command armies or impose sanctions, but it maintains diplomatic missions, quiet back channels, and relationships with parties that refuse to speak to one another publicly.

The piece argues that this quiet power is underrated. In an era of loud summitry and performative diplomacy, the Vatican can sometimes move hostages, prisoners, or ceasefires because it is not trying to win a news cycle. Its long time horizon and non-state status let it absorb failures without losing face.

That does not make it neutral. Papal diplomacy has real interests: protecting Catholic institutions, preserving access to vulnerable populations, and promoting a particular vision of peace. But the style is deliberately different—slower, quieter, less visible.

**Why this matters:** When official diplomacy becomes theater, the back channel that actually moves may be the one nobody is watching.

### 6. Getting Good: Effective Altruism confronts its own culture

The Point has published a long reported essay by Harry Levov on Effective Altruism, framed around the EA Global conference in New York in October 2025. The piece is less about whether EA’s calculations are correct and more about what happens when a movement built on ruthless cause prioritization becomes an institution.

The tension Levov identifies is familiar from earlier social movements. Early EA was lean, contrarian, and comfortable with uncomfortable conclusions. Now it has think tanks, donors, career pipelines, and a public image problem. The essay suggests the movement is grappling with human messiness: status, ambition, belonging, and the desire to be seen as good.

That matters because EA has shaped billions of dollars in funding and increasingly shapes AI policy conversations. If its internal culture cannot govern itself, its external recommendations become easier to dismiss.

**Why this matters:** A movement that claims to be guided by evidence will be judged not only by its spreadsheets, but by how it actually treats people.

## Paper of the Day

### Harm Laundering in GPT Models

Today’s paper asks a quietly devastating question: when safety scores improve, what actually got safer?

The paper is by Sarah Wyer [WY-er], Sue Black, and Noura Al Moubayed [NOO-rah al moo-BYE-ed], on arXiv [archive]. They analyzed 450,000 gender-directed completions across 15 models spanning GPT-2 through GPT-5. The official story in AI safety is that explicit discriminatory content declines across generations. Their claim is that this is an illusion created by surface-form evaluation. They call it **harm laundering**.

The story they tell goes like this. In GPT-2, women-directed output contained clusters of sexual violence. By GPT-4, those clusters largely disappear. That looks like progress. But look at what happens to men-directed output. It gains positive representational territory—caregiving, emotional range, ally identity—that women-directed output does not get. The discrimination does not vanish; it changes shape.

The most striking result is at GPT-5. One topic cluster, with 1,997 documents, frames breast cancer as a men’s rights debate. There is zero equivalent cluster in women-directed output. Three independent toxicity classifiers score that content as non-toxic. The classifiers are not catching the problem because the problem is no longer a slur; it is an asymmetric representational choice.

Sentiment scores invert at GPT-4. Early models demean women. Later models over-correct. Topic diversity in women-directed completions falls 36 percent relative to men at the GPT-4 alignment boundary. The model becomes more careful with one group and more expansive with another.

The so-what is sharp. If you evaluate safety by looking for toxic words, you reward models that learn to launder harm into subtler forms. Product teams cannot trust a declining toxicity score. They need representation-aware audits that ask not just “is this toxic?” but “whose stories are being expanded, and whose are being narrowed?”

**Why this matters:** Safety metrics that only catch the last generation’s harms will systematically miss the next generation’s methods.

## One Idea

### Vary the retrieval cue, not just the content

Here is an idea you can use this afternoon. It comes from a set of experiments by Andrew Butler and colleagues, covered this week by Megan Sumeracki [soo-mer-ACK-ee] at The Learning Scientists.

The setup is simple. Undergraduate students watched short video clips about science topics. Then they answered application questions from memory. There were two conditions. In the “same” condition, students applied each concept to the same example three times. In the “variable” condition, they applied the concept to different examples each time.

The result was clear. When students practiced retrieval with varied examples, they were much better at transferring the concept to a completely new example later. Practicing with the same example felt fluent. The varied version felt harder. But the harder version produced the kind of understanding that moves.

The mechanism is not mysterious. If you always retrieve a concept using the same example, you can succeed by remembering the example itself. You do not have to extract the underlying rule. But if the example changes every time, the only stable thing left is the concept. Variation forces your brain to find the invariant principle behind the surface differences.

Psychologists call this a desirable difficulty. It is difficult because it feels worse in the moment. It is desirable because it produces better retention and transfer. The feeling of ease is, in this case, a trap.

How do you actually use this?

In conversation, do not rehearse your favorite argument only in the form you first heard it. Apply it to a current event, a different industry, a historical case. If you can explain the same idea through three different windows, you own it.

In your own research, when you review a paper, do not just recall the finding. Ask: what would happen if I applied this mechanism to a completely different domain? Does the logic hold? If not, was the original appeal just surface familiarity?

In study, make flashcards that force variation. Instead of asking “What is X?” three times, ask “How does X explain Y?” where Y changes. The prompt should vary enough that you cannot rely on a memorized trigger.

The same logic applies to how you teach or brief a team. One example is an anecdote. Three varied examples are a pattern.

**Why this matters:** The work that feels worse in the moment is often the work that sticks—if your practice is smooth, you are probably practicing recognition, not retrieval.
