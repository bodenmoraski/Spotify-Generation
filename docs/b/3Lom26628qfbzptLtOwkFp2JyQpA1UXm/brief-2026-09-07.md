## The World

### 1. Capital gains vs. wealth taxes

Tyler Cowen at Marginal Revolution flags a new Econometrica [ih-kon-uh-MEH-trik-uh] paper by Mark Aguiar [ah-GHEE-ar], Benjamin Moll [mohl], and Florian Scheuer [SHOY-er] that tries to settle an old fiscal fight: capital gains versus wealth taxes. The standard theory, they argue, has a blind spot. It models capital income but not the actual prices of assets. In modern finance, asset prices move for two reasons: changes in expected cash flows, and changes in discount rates — basically shifts in sentiment or risk appetite. So a stock can double without the company producing a single extra dollar. The authors show that a combination of realization-based capital gains taxes and cash-flow taxes can implement the optimal allocation no matter what drove the price move. The clever part: their capital gains tax targets total net trades, not gains from selling individual assets, so it avoids the lock-in effect that punishes people for not selling winners. In this setup, wealth taxes lose the comparison, because they tax paper wealth before it is realized and distort decisions more severely. The paper is technical, but the headline is plain: if you want to tax the kind of wealth that jumps around because the market changed its mood, a well-designed capital gains tax beats an annual wealth tax. **Why this matters:** If wealth taxes are structurally inferior for taxing asset-price swings, the current global debate about taxing unrealized gains needs a different starting point.

### 2. Closed airspace turns geopolitics into a climate cost

Brazilian business coverage has been translating a quiet shift: airspace closure. Forbes Brasil’s piece — “Fechamento do Espaço Aéreo Está Prolongando Voos e Transformando a Geopolítica em Problema Climático” — argues that closed air corridors are no longer just an aviation nuisance. Every time a conflict or territorial dispute closes a sector, flights get rerouted around it. Those extra kilometers burn extra fuel. Multiply one reroute by thousands of flights a month, and the climate cost becomes a standing, unlegislated carbon tax imposed by geopolitics. The piece’s point is systemic: aviation’s carbon problem is usually discussed as technology and efficiency, but here sovereignty itself is producing emissions. A plane avoiding a hot zone is not choosing to pollute more; the map forced it. And because airspace closures are decided by defense ministries, not climate negotiators, the climate cost is never counted in either the war budget or the carbon budget. Whether the closure is over the Sahel [sah-HEL], the Black Sea, or parts of the Middle East, the economics are similar once you connect the dots. **Why this matters:** It recasts borders and airspace as climate infrastructure — closed skies are a slow, unaccounted emissions policy.

### 3. Latin America flirts with guerrilla geopolitics

The Japan Times asks whether Latin America will embrace what it calls “guerrilla geopolitics.” The phrase is more provocation than analysis, but it names a real change. Instead of picking a side between Washington and Beijing, Latin American governments are increasingly playing small, cheap, hard-to-reverse moves: signing one-off infrastructure deals, opening ports to specific navies, trading mining concessions for digital loans. The piece suggests this is not neutrality but a form of leverage — using great-power competition to extract better terms without joining a bloc. The question is whether the region can keep that game going without becoming a battlefield. Lithium, copper, rare earths, and now data cables make the region impossible to ignore. One day it is a Chinese-run deepwater port in Peru; the next it is a U.S. tariff on Mexican steel. Read separately, they look like isolated deals. Read together, they are moves in a longer, messier game. **Why this matters:** If Latin America perfects the art of the guerrilla maneuver, the next decade’s supply chains and alliances will be more transactional, more reversible, and much harder to map.

### 4. OpenAI funds Ukrainian newsrooms — and raises the independence question

OpenAI announced a program with AIRPPU [air-POO] and WAN-IFRA [wahn IF-rah] to support independent journalism in Ukraine, focused on innovation, resilience, and digital tools. It is easy to read this as a press release, and mostly it is. But the structural question underneath is worth naming. A company that trains its models on the open web, including news text, is now a patron of newsrooms. The same firm that benefits from scraping the web is funding the people who fill it. That creates a dependency: newsrooms in a war zone may come to rely on AI-lab money for survival. The program may be genuinely useful — Ukrainian journalism needs infrastructure and tools, especially under bombardment — but the word “independent” is doing heavy lifting when your patron has a commercial interest in how AI is covered and in keeping the web full of high-quality text to ingest. This is not a reason to reject the money. It is a reason to watch what the money asks for later. **Why this matters:** AI firms are becoming media patrons; the flow of money will eventually shape the flow of questions about AI itself.

### 5. Cartography as an old argument that still shapes policy

The New Indian Express returns to the Mercator [mur-KAY-tor] projection and the politics of how we see the world. Mercator makes Greenland look roughly the size of Africa, when Africa is about fourteen times larger. This is not a new observation. But the piece argues it still matters because maps are not neutral wall art; they are mental infrastructure. Aid budgets, trade routes, military attention, even pandemic response follow the mental map before the actual one. When a continent is visually shrunk, its problems and markets look smaller too. A policymaker who grows up with a distorted default map may underweight African markets or overestimate the strategic importance of northern territories. The piece does not break news. But it is a useful reminder that the first policy choice is often what you put in the center of the frame — and what you push to the edge. **Why this matters:** Changing the default map projection is a cheap intervention with real geopolitical consequences, which is why the old argument keeps returning.

## Paper of the Day

### Same trajectory, contradictory rewards

Imagine a robot arm. A human says, “pass me the cup.” The arm reaches, grips, extends. The vision-language model watching says: success, reward 0.9. Now the human says the same task slightly differently: “hand me the mug.” The motion is identical. The model says: failure, reward 0.2. That is not a hypothetical; it is the central finding from a new paper on arXiv [archive], titled “Same Trajectory, Contradictory Rewards (ROBORMBENCH): Paraphrase Fragility in Vision Language Reward Models.”

The authors ask a very simple question: should a robot be scored the same when the instruction is paraphrased but the action is the same? The answer seems obviously yes. Yet current vision-language models, or V-L-Ms [vee-ell-emz], often violate that property. They are being used as reward functions for robotic learning, but they are paraphrase-fragile. Change a word, and the numerical progress score shifts. Often it flips. The same trajectory can be labeled success or failure depending only on the phrasing.

To measure this, the researchers built ROBORMBENCH — a benchmark with 2,390 real-robot trajectories, ground-truth progress labels, and 21,673 verified paraphrases spanning lexical, syntactic, and action-goal rewrites. Across proprietary and open-source V-L-Ms, paraphrase-induced instability is widespread and severe. It grows under more divergent rewrites. And here is the kicker: scale and explicit reasoning do not reliably reduce it. You can take a bigger model, or ask it to think step by step, and it will still flip its judgment because you changed one word.

Dedicated reward models trained with trajectory-grounded supervision are substantially more stable. That points to a fix: don’t grab a general V-L-M off the shelf and call it a judge. Train it on robot trajectories with explicit progress signals. The failure mode is subtle because the model is not blind to the robot; it sees the video. It just weighs language too much. A paraphrase changes what it attends to, and the reward collapses. **Why this matters:** If a reward model can be fooled by a synonym, then robot learning in the wild is being guided by a grader that doesn’t actually understand the assignment.

## One Idea

### When wealth can be monopolized, strongmen follow

The Nautilus piece “What Archaeology Reveals About the Rise of All-Powerful Rulers” runs a sharp counterfactual through ancient history. Autocracy was not inevitable. Many early civilizations — including some large, complex settlements — were strikingly egalitarian for centuries. They built big architecture, coordinated irrigation, ran trade networks, all without a single ruler hoarding power. Then something changed, but not because human nature suddenly became selfish. The pattern is more specific: when riches appeared that could be monopolized — a concentrated source of metal, a choke point on a trade route, a surplus that could be stored and guarded — strongmen emerged to control it. Where resources were scattered or required collective maintenance, flatter hierarchies held.

The article’s own summary is blunt: “Autocracy wasn’t inevitable in early civilizations—many were more egalitarian. But when riches were there to monopolize, strongmen and inequalities emerged.” That is a mental model you can carry. Whenever you see a concentration of power, ask where the monopolizable resource is. In a company, it might be access to a key client. In a research field, it might be control of a dataset. In a nation, it might be a mineral deposit or a port. The mechanism is the same: resource concentration creates a prize; a prize attracts someone willing to take it; once taken, it funds further centralization. The implication for design is to diffuse the prize, not just lament the strongman. If you want less autocracy, make the resource harder to capture. **Why this matters:** This flips the old debate — if inequality is not a law of nature, the thing to study is the infrastructure of monopolization, not the moral character of rulers.

### The Vitamin: a single-subject, AI-assisted self-experiment

On LessWrong, Shoshannah Tekofsky [sho-SHAH-nah teh-KOF-skee] wrote about “The Vitamin” — her personal discovery of 10 grams a day of glycine [GLY-seen], which over six months improved her sleep quality and tolerance for sleep deprivation. The essay is not a clinical study. It is a stylized self-experiment, and the author is explicit that she prompted AI models with her “sleep dealio” until one suggested glycine. The useful idea here is not glycine itself; it is the method of small, tracked, single-subject experiments.

Define the problem precisely. Generate hypotheses from an LLM or the literature. Test one intervention at a time. Track subjective and objective measures. Accept that your result may not generalize. This is deliberately low-certainty, high-personal-return science. It will not prove anything for anyone else, but it can change your own baseline. The trap is fooling yourself, so a placebo-free personal trial needs honest tracking and a willingness to reverse the intervention if the effect fades. **Why this matters:** In a world of universal health advice, the hardest skill is not finding information but running a controlled experiment on yourself without believing your own story too fast.
