## The World

Five items today, moving from economic scenarios to constitutional argument to the quieter channels of diplomacy.

### 1. AI’s economic future is being turned into scenario tables, not forecasts

Start with a paper that is less a prediction than a scenario machine. Tyler Cowen [KOW-en] points to new work from Anton Korinek [KOR-in-ek], Charles I. Jones, Szymon Sacher [SHIH-mon SAH-ker], Tess Cotter, and Peter McCrory [muh-KROR-ee], several of them at Anthropic. They build a model of the United States economy from 2026 to 2030 in which AI automates a growing share of cognitive work. The model tracks productivity, GDP, the share of income going to labor, wages, how many workers have to switch occupations, and unemployment.

Then they run three scenarios: modest, substantial, and extreme. In the modest case, AI adds less than half a point to annual GDP growth by 2030 and pushes unemployment up by a tenth of a point. In the extreme case, AI performs almost half of today’s cognitive work by 2030, GDP growth rises to 15 percent per year, the labor share of income falls from 60 percent to 45 percent, and nearly one in five cognitive workers is unemployed.

That is not a point forecast. It is a coherent path from capability to consequence. If you think models plateau, you buy the modest scenario. If you think they keep scaling, you have to explain why the labor-share drop does not happen. The authors also survey US adults about their expectations, and the gap between public expectation and the extreme scenario is itself news.

**Why this matters:** The argument moves the AI fight from “will it replace us?” to “which transition path are we already on?”

### 2. China’s humanoid robot lead may be more about cost culture than subsidies

To China, where ChinaTalk has published an abridged translation of a Caijing [tsai-JING] magazine profile of Unitree [YOO-nee-tree], the humanoid robot company. The piece is built on a simple observation: CEO Wang Xingxing [WONG SHING-shing] is obsessed with cost. Not in the abstract — down to components, assembly, materials, and supply chains. The profile walks through how Unitree brought humanoid robots onto the Spring Festival Gala stage, and how the company’s famously low sticker price is not a marketing trick. It comes from a managerial culture that treats every unnecessary dollar as a form of failure.

The profile also covers the IPO and how it has — and has not — changed employees’ lives. The larger story is less about one company than about a strategic style. China’s robotics push is often framed in Washington as a story about state subsidies. This reporting suggests the subsidy story is incomplete. There is also a brutally effective cost culture that many Western hardware startups simply cannot match. That matters for anyone thinking about who will dominate physical AI, logistics, elder care, and eventually the home.

**Why this matters:** If the next wave of AI is embodied — robots, not chatbots — the cost per useful act may matter more than the model size.

### 3. Jill Lepore argues the Constitution was built to be amended, not worshipped

Now a cultural argument with constitutional stakes. Jill Lepore [leh-PORE] has a long essay in Literary Hub called “Neither Bone Nor Stone,” and the title already tells you the thesis. She begins with the actual material of the US Constitution: parchment made from sheep, ink from oak galls swollen by wasp eggs, letters shaped by goose quills. The point of the physical description is not poetry. It is to insist that the Constitution is an artifact made by living people, not a stone tablet.

Lepore quotes James Madison’s failed amendment from 1789 — that the people have an indubitable, unalienable, and indefeasible right to reform or change their government whenever it is inadequate. She uses that to push against the originalist habit of treating the founding text as frozen. The essay arrives at a moment when constitutional amendment in the United States is nearly impossible in practice, which makes the argument much less academic than it sounds. If a document cannot be updated, every dispute becomes a fight over what the original text secretly meant.

**Why this matters:** It changes the question from “what did the framers think?” to “why did the framers build in a way to revise themselves?”

### 4. A defense of the university as a craft, not a platform

A shorter but sharp essay in The Point by Campbell Scribner [SKRIB-ner] asks what academics owe when public institutions are under pressure. His term is “internal goods”: the value of clear thinking, shared inquiry, and intellectual craft, rather than external metrics. The essay is occasioned by a specific institutional crisis, but the move is general.

Scribner argues that if scholars want to defend universities, they have to actually practice the values they claim to protect. Not perform outrage, but do the slow work of making distinctions, testing arguments, and refusing to flatten complexity. It is a defense of the academy as a place where shared value can be sustained, not as a platform for position-taking. It pairs oddly well with the Lepore piece: both are about institutions that survive by being revisable, not by being rigid.

**Why this matters:** In a climate where universities are attacked from outside and hollowed from inside, the only durable defense is the craft itself.

### 5. When official channels freeze, Track Two keeps a door open

Finally, a quieter global item. The Nuclear Threat Initiative has a piece on Track Two diplomacy — the unofficial, semi-official dialogues that continue when formal channels close. The headline point is simple: when official communication freezes, someone still needs to keep talking. Track Two is not a replacement for real diplomacy. It is a pressure release and a pre-negotiation.

The piece is light on new specifics, but the timing is the signal. With several major-power relationships running cold, the mechanisms that kept communication alive during past crises are being rebuilt. If you hear about a retired ambassador or a former minister at a conference table in a third country, that is not nostalgia. That is often the only channel left.

**Why this matters:** Wars and sanctions often end not when leaders meet, but when someone has kept enough informal contact alive to make a meeting possible.

## Paper of the Day

### A positive resolution of the gap-entropy conjecture

Today’s paper is a detective story about how many samples you need to find the best answer. Imagine you have ten online ad variants, ten clinical treatment protocols, or ten robot policies — and each time you test one, you pay money or time. You want to find the single best option with high confidence, then stop. This is called best-arm identification.

The hard part is intuitive: if the best option and the second-best option are extremely close, deciding between them requires a huge number of samples. If there is a wide gap, you can stop sooner. For years, the standard formula said the total sample complexity scales with the sum, over every losing arm, of one divided by the square of its gap from the best. Call that sum H. A bigger gap means one over gap squared is small; smaller gaps explode.

But there was a nagging conjecture: H alone might be enough only if the gaps are clustered at a single scale. If you have one arm that is almost tied, another slightly worse, another much worse, you face many different levels of difficulty at once. The conjecture said you also need an entropy term: the Shannon entropy of the relative contributions to H. Entropy here measures how spread out the difficulties are. If one arm dominates H, entropy is low. If many arms contribute evenly at different scales, entropy is high, and you need more samples.

This paper by Aronow [AIR-oh-now], Kallus [KAL-us], and Lopatto [loh-PAH-toh] on arXiv [archive] proves the conjecture. For independent Gaussian arms with means between zero and one and a unique best arm, the optimal expected number of samples is within a constant factor of H times the log of one over the failure probability, plus that entropy term. They also construct an algorithm that achieves the bound up to a small extra term involving the closest gap.

The so-what: if you are running adaptive experiments — clinical trials, A/B tests, AI model evaluations — you should expect more cost when the field of alternatives is not uniformly weak but variably weak. You cannot just budget by the hardest pair. The distribution of gaps itself is information.

**Why this matters:** This sharpens a directly usable rule for experimenters: your sample budget depends not only on how close the top contenders are, but on how many different closeness scales your problem contains.

## One Idea

### Why a proof can be true and still not satisfy

Today’s idea is about the difference between verifying a claim and understanding it. A new proof of the four-color theorem, reported this week by Quanta [KWAHN-tah] Magazine, makes the distinction unusually clean.

The four-color theorem says that any flat map — countries, counties, scribbles — can be colored with only four colors so that no two adjacent regions share a color. It was proved in 1976 by Kenneth Appel [ah-PEL] and Wolfgang Haken [HAH-ken], but the proof used a computer to check nearly two thousand cases. Mathematicians accepted the result, but many felt cheated. The proof verified the theorem without explaining why four colors are enough. It was a certificate of truth, not a theory. The new proof reported by Quanta is notable precisely because it tries to close that explanatory gap — to make the result feel inevitable rather than merely checked.

That distinction is a mental model you can use far from topology. A lot of what we call learning is verification. You reread a paragraph and it feels familiar; that is your brain checking a stored pattern. You see a regression coefficient and a p-value; that verifies an association. You hear a claim and it matches your priors; that verifies a tribal position. None of that tells you why the result had to be that way, or what would break it.

Understanding is different. It means you can regenerate the claim from simpler principles. You can explain it to someone with no notes. You can predict where it might fail. The itch mathematicians feel about Appel and Haken is the same itch you should feel when a result is true but unmotivated: the explanation has not yet been found.

There is a direct way to use this. In conversation, stop asking, “Is that true?” and start asking, “Why would that have to be true?” If someone cannot answer, the claim may still be true, but it is not yet understood. In your own research, when you get a result, write one sentence beginning, “This had to happen because…” If you cannot finish that sentence, you have verification without explanation. In study, replace rereading with retrieval: close the document and try to rebuild the argument from the pieces. The feeling of difficulty is the explanation being installed.

The medieval mental-health essay from Aeon [EE-on] makes the same move against presentism. The standard story is that medieval mental-health care was superstition: saffron, ostrich liver. But Katherine Harvey points out that the same sources prescribe exercise, gardens, animals, socializing, and music. Our ancestors were not just failed moderns with bad drugs. They had a different explanatory model of the mind and body. Once you see their logic, the remedies stop being a list of oddities and become a coherent system. That is the same shift from verifying a fact — medieval people did weird things — to understanding why their world made sense to them.

**Why this matters:** The feeling of ease is often just verification; the harder work of explanation is what survives.
