## The World

### 1. The data center is a symbol

Dan Kagan-Kans, writing in Transformer, says the usual case against data centers is full of claims that dissolve on contact: water use, energy per AI query, a nine-degree heat island. Data centers use far less water than golf courses, AI queries now often use the same energy as a Google search, and the nine-degree figure is absurd. Yet the protests keep coming. Kagan-Kans argues that’s because the data center has become a symbol, not a factual dispute. Under the hysterical claims there is an emotional signal. The real objection is to a landscape suddenly occupied by opaque infrastructure nobody in the community chose: decisions made elsewhere, resources redirected, a future imposed from outside. The data center is the visible tip of that bargain. If you answer the false numbers, you miss the point. The essay’s lesson: when a debate seems immune to facts, look for the symbolic function of the object. Here, the data center stands for loss of control, not for water tables.

**Why this matters:** It changes how you read every local backlash against AI, from data centers to model training to autonomous vehicles: the stated costs are often a proxy for a deeper argument about power.

### 2. Brazil’s Amazon rare earths become a sovereignty fight

Brazilian outlets, including Portal Você Online, are framing the Mineração Taboca [mee-neh-rah-SAOW tah-BOH-kah] rare earth deposit in Amazonas as the new center of a global tug-of-war. The non-Anglophone coverage focuses less on the mine itself and more on who benefits. Chinese firms already dominate rare earth processing, and the prospect of deeper involvement in Brazil’s Amazon reserves is being read as an attempt to lock in the choke point before Western buyers can line up alternatives. Local reporting asks harder questions than the wires: about environmental damage, indigenous land rights, and whether royalties will stay in Amazonas or flow to Brasília and Beijing. That is a very different frame from the supply-chain puzzle story, which treats rare earths as an abstraction. The local version is about sovereignty, land, and who gets to say no.

**Why this matters:** Rare earths are the substrate of motors, magnets, and defense systems. The story is not just about minerals but about whether a developing country can set terms for extraction or is treated as a resource fortress for larger powers.

### 3. Pakistan’s military codifies power while bracing for backlash

Pakistan is deploying more troops in Rawalpindi [rah-wahl-PIN-dee] after granting sweeping new powers to army chief General Asim Munir [AH-sim moo-NEER], according to ThePrint. The deployment is being read as a hedge against backlash, not as a routine security measure. That tells you the actual story. The military is codifying its authority over civilian functions while preparing for the political cost. Rawalpindi is the garrison city; troop movements there are never neutral. For Pakistani politics, the question is no longer whether the military matters, but whether the last levers of civilian oversight can survive the formal expansion of military power. Regional press treats it as a slow-motion constitutional shift. The formal legal expansion is the point, not the threat of street protest.

**Why this matters:** Pakistan is a nuclear state with a young population and persistent economic stress. A further entrenchment of military control changes the risk calculus for India, China, and the Gulf states.

### 4. Birding as training for solidarity

Megan Cummins, writing in Public Books, is pushing back on the idea that birding is an escape from social life. The essay, “Birds Beyond Birding,” argues that looking at birds can be a discipline for noticing connections: migration routes that cross borders, habitats that depend on collective decisions, species whose survival is tied to policies made far away. The quote to remember: “Looking at birds helps us notice connections, imagine better worlds, and build new solidarities across differences, including across species.” That is not a field guide; it is a political epistemology. The practice becomes less about listing rare sightings and more about training attention on interdependence. A bird is not an object to check off, but a relation.

**Why this matters:** It gives you a different way to talk about hobbies at lunch. When someone mentions birding as a quiet hobby, you can say it is actually a training ground for noticing how systems connect, and maybe the most radical thing a walk outside can do is remind you that you are not alone in the world.

### 5. Talking about death is an attention hack

Literary Hub has an essay by E.K. Schrader that is not really about death; it is about attention. Schrader describes realizing as a child that she would die, and then later teaching alongside the poet Max Ritvo [RIT-voh] before his death from cancer. The argument is that talking about death is not morbid; it is a way to live with a clearer allocation of energy. The essay pushes against a culture that hides mortality inside hospital systems and life-extension fantasies. Once you say the sentence out loud—“I am going to die”—the trivial drama of the week gets simpler. A lot of what feels urgent loses its urgency. The essay does not offer a checklist; it offers a tone.

**Why this matters:** In a moment when AI promises to extend cognition and longevity, the missing conversation is finitude. The ability to talk about death may be the cheapest life hack available.

## Paper of the Day

### Phantom Gains: Auditing Self-Improvement Against a Measured Null

The paper, from Cheng Xu and collaborators, asks a simple question: when a language model appears to improve itself, how do we know the improvement is real?

Here is the story. Increasingly, people don’t judge a language model by its average accuracy. They judge it by which individual problems it gains and loses after some self-training step. But tracking those transitions means subtracting two noisy estimates. And subtracting noisy estimates is exactly where artifacts appear.

So the team did something almost too obvious. They took a Qwen3-8B model, ran three rounds of LoRA [LOW-rah] self-training, and compared it against a frozen control pushed through the identical pipeline. The control did not train at all. But the standard ledger still manufactured capability changes on the untrained model. A single greedy decode created apparent gains, largely because of something as ordinary as inference batching. An expansion statistic, the kind that separates acquisition from sharpening, assigned the frozen model a rate of 0.28. Seven measurement failures, each one severe enough to invert a reported finding when the control was absent. Several are standard practice.

The team then replaced the fragile threshold repair with a per-problem exact test against a pooled baseline, under false-discovery-rate control. That test detected nothing on any held-out replicate. It stayed unchanged under different multiple-testing rules, error rates, and pool sizes.

The so-what is not just about one model. The paper names a quiet failure mode in AI evaluation: we are counting phantom gains. If you read that a model improved itself through self-training, ask whether the authors ran a frozen control through the same pipeline. If not, the gains may be measurement artifacts.

**Why this matters:** This is a directly usable skepticism. The next time you see a self-improvement claim, you do not need to understand every statistic. You just need to ask: where is the control?

## One Idea

### Unwanted ease, applied

Here is a pattern that should change how you use AI.

Althea Need Kaminske [AL-thee-ah need kuh-MIN-skee] at The Learning Scientists points to two studies. In one, Chinese students in grades 7 through 12 who used generative AI heavily on homework scored higher on homework but lower on exams. Students who used little or no AI, or used it strategically, did worse on homework but better on exams. In another study, adults solving fraction problems with an AI assistant available outperformed a no-AI group for the first twelve problems. Then the assistant was removed without warning. For the last three problems, the pattern reversed.

Kaminske says this is a familiar memory result. Conditions that feel easy initially do not necessarily produce durable learning. The mechanism is retrieval. Whenever the answer is supplied or generated before you struggle, your brain does not have to retrieve. Retrieval is the thing that changes memory. This is a specific case of desirable difficulties. Making the task slightly harder at the moment of practice improves later performance. The feeling of ease is not a sign of learning; it is a sign that the work is being offloaded.

How to use it. If you use a language model to help you read a paper or write an email, do the first pass yourself. Write the ugly draft. Generate the summary. Attempt the problem. Then consult the model. You can even ask it not to give the answer, but to ask you the next question. In conversation, resist the urge to let a model autocomplete your point. If you cannot say the idea out loud, you do not own it. The awkward pause is the learning episode. In your own research, when you use an AI assistant to generate code or a literature review, schedule a retrieval check. After a day, close the assistant and write down what you actually understood. Treat the gap as information, not shame.

Kaminske’s title, “Unwanted Ease,” is the point. The ease is wanted in the moment and unwanted in the long run. The work that feels worse is often the work that sticks.

**Why this matters:** As AI becomes more capable, the cost of easy performance is hidden. The students who let AI do their homework looked better until the exam. The people who will thrive in an AI-saturated workplace may be the ones who deliberately do things the hard way.

And a smaller idea from Quanta [KWAN-tah] this week. It is about the math of imbalance. The simple task of splitting people or objects into two fair groups is hard. A new breakthrough in discrepancy theory gives better tools for how much imbalance is unavoidable. The portable use: when a problem feels easy but keeps producing slightly unfair outcomes, you may be facing a deeper mathematical structure, not a lack of effort. Stop tweaking. Change the method.
