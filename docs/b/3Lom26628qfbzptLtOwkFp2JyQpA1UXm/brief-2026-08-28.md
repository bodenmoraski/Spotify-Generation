## The World

### 1. The U.S. and China are now fighting a meme war

A sharp piece from ChinaTalk by Karuna Nandkumar [kuh-ROO-nah NAND-koo-mar] argues that U.S.-China military competition has entered a new domain: meme warfare. Not memes as a side effect of propaganda, but memes as a deliberate layer over state violence.

The piece begins with a strange premonition: the Texas judge who accidentally appeared in a Zoom trial with a cat filter during COVID. At the time it was a joke. Now, Chinese state media is layering cute animal filters onto ballistic missiles, and the White House social team is splicing bombing footage with SpongeBob clips. The point is not that missiles have become funny. It is that the filter domesticates the weapon. When military action becomes shareable content, the emotional register changes from horror to familiarity.

Nandkumar’s argument is that both countries are building a filter of ease and glamour over the harsh realities of war. The meme is not a replacement for the missile; it is preparation for it. It trains audiences to scroll past violence without the normal shock.

**Why this matters:** The battlefield is now partly semantic: whoever controls the filter controls how much violence feels normal.

### 2. Iran keeps a diplomatic door open while threatening the Strait of Hormuz [hor-MOOZ]

Brazilian business coverage is giving this story a different weight from the Washington read. The headline from Times Brasil and CNBC: Iran says diplomacy with the United States is still possible, but threatens offensive actions in the Strait of Hormuz.

The oscillation matters more than either half of the sentence. Tehran is keeping a diplomatic off-ramp visible while simultaneously raising the cost of any military pressure in the Strait. That is a classic move: do not close the door, but make sure the other side knows how expensive it will be to push through it.

For Brazil, the framing is less about nuclear posture and more about commodity logistics. A major commodity exporter watches the Strait because war-risk insurance and tanker rates reprice instantly on a threat. The local angle is not “will Iran do it,” but “who pays the freight premium if the threat merely persists.”

**Why this matters:** The threat itself is a pricing signal: it makes escalation expensive before it even begins.

### 3. Nepal is rejecting foreign rescue teams after a flood, and the question is why

Normally, after catastrophic flooding, governments accept every set of foreign hands. So the Indian-sourced coverage in Modern Diplomacy is asking an uncomfortable question: why is Nepal reportedly refusing foreign rescue teams?

The likely answer is not logistics. It is sovereignty and narrative control. Accepting rescue teams means accepting the storyline that the state cannot handle its own emergency. For a small country wedged between India and China, refusing outside teams can also be a way of refusing to choose a patron publicly. Accept a Chinese team and Delhi notices. Accept an Indian team and Beijing notices. Refuse both, and you preserve a fragile neutrality even while the water is rising.

This is the part of disaster politics that gets lost in the wire ledes: crises are not just humanitarian events. They are competitions over legitimacy. When a government says no to helicopters and divers, it is making a claim about who is allowed to save its citizens.

**Why this matters:** Disaster response is a sovereignty claim made under maximum pressure, not just a relief operation.

### 4. Think people hate AI now? Wait until it takes their jobs

The Transformer newsletter has an argument that should cut through the current data-center fight. Yes, the public hates data centers. But the piece points out that data centers have relatively little direct material impact on most people. You can hate them from a distance. Jobs are different.

The argument is that the coming wave of AI-driven job displacement will produce a backlash far more personal than anything we have seen over power lines and water use. Anthropic’s giant IPO may grab the headlines, but the harder question sits underneath: what happens when a technology that is currently visible mostly through its infrastructure becomes visible through layoffs?

People can ignore a data center. They cannot ignore a lost shift. The current fight is about environmental externalities and land use. The next fight will be about legitimacy: who benefits from this technology, and who bears the cost? If AI proponents treat public anger as a permitting problem, they will be unprepared for a payroll problem.

**Why this matters:** The real legitimacy crisis for AI will not be about electricity; it will be about income.

### 5. Patrick Collison’s argument that culture is stuck because it lost the sacred

Luke Burgis interviews Patrick Collison [KOL-ih-son] about aesthetics, and the conversation moves quickly from taste to something much larger. Collison’s claim is that culture is stuck: a 1926 Bauhaus [BOW-house] building could pass for last year’s design. Modernism’s explicit mission, he argues, was to tear down beauty — and they were not hiding it.

The interview picks up a Solzhenitsyn [sohl-zhe-NYI-tsin] line: you can fake goodness and truth, but beauty bears within it its own verification. Collison’s test is simple. Why does everyone visit Toledo, Spain, and no one visits Toledo, Ohio? Because beauty is not purely subjective. People vote with their travel.

Then the sharper observation: Detroit was rich for about twenty-five years and built the Detroit Institute of Arts. Silicon Valley has been rich for fifty, and, as Collison says, you could do the Silicon Valley art tour in a coffee break. The deepest claim is that around 1971 the sacred got lost, and with it a shared reason to build beautiful things. If beauty verifies itself, then the problem is not just underfunding of artists; it is that institutions no longer treat beauty as something real enough to be accountable to.

**Why this matters:** The stagnation argument moves from taste to structure: you cannot grant your way out of a lost sense of the sacred.

## Paper of the Day

### LeVJEPA: Efficient and Scalable Video Pretraining without the Heuristics

Today’s paper is from the arXiv [archive], and it asks a question that sounds technical but is really about simplicity. Can we teach a machine to see movement without all the scaffolding that has made video learning so expensive and fragile?

The paper is called LeVJEPA [lev-JEH-puh] — short for Efficient and Scalable Video Pretraining without the Heuristics. The authors include Yann LeCun [yahn luh-KUN], one of the people who built modern deep learning, and the result is oddly elegant.

Here is the problem. Video carries the temporal structure of the physical world: gravity, inertia, object permanence. But training a network on video is computationally enormous. To keep the network from collapsing — from ignoring its inputs and spitting out the same representation — the dominant self-supervised methods rely on architectural asymmetries. That usually means a slowly updated target encoder, a stop-gradient, a capacity-limited predictor, or reconstructing masked pixels in pixel space. Those tricks work, but they are expensive and finicky.

The paper asks: what if we just drop them?

LeVJEPA trains a single encoder with an invariance loss over global and local views of a clip. Think of it as asking the network to agree with itself when it sees the whole scene and when it sees a tight crop. The collapse problem is handled by a regularizer called SIGReg [SIG-rej], which comes with a provable guarantee. No target encoder. No stop-gradient. No pixel reconstruction. Just an encoder, a projector, and one hyperparameter.

The clever part is token dropping. During pretraining, the model uniformly drops random tokens before the encoder sees them. Because the cost is governed by how many tokens the encoder actually observes, dropping tokens makes training cheap. And it does not just save compute; it actually improves downstream accuracy. At matched epochs on identical data, LeVJEPA matches or surpasses V-JEPA 2 [VEE-jeh-puh two] across a range of model sizes while using between 5.6 and 20.8 times less pretraining compute.

The so-what is not just an engineering win. The field has assumed for years that you need the heuristics to avoid collapse. This paper suggests the heuristics were costly crutches. The dial that matters is not primarily model size or dataset size, but how many tokens you actually observe. That is a different mental model for video pretraining, and for world models more broadly: less can be more if the objective is well-regularized. If video understanding is a step toward machines that can forecast physical events, cutting compute by an order of magnitude opens the door to many more labs.

**Why this matters:** Efficiency is not just engineering; it determines who gets to build the systems that learn how the physical world behaves.

## One Idea

Two ideas today. They sound unrelated, but both are about not mistaking the tool for the thing itself.

First, from Quanta, a piece built around Edsger Dijkstra [EDZ-ger DIKE-struh], the Turing Award-winning computer scientist. His line is famous: computer science is no more about computers than astronomy is about telescopes. The discipline is about computation, abstraction, and reasoning. The machine is just the instrument.

The piece asks whether computer science education needs computers at all — or at least whether hands-on programming should be the first gate. Dijkstra was famously hostile to certain programming languages, but the deeper point is not crankiness. It is that if you teach the tool first, students may mistake fluency with the tool for understanding the field. The portable version: ask what you are actually trying to teach or learn. If the goal is problem decomposition, then the terminal is one way but not the only way. If the goal is vocational training, fine — but call it that.

In your own research, do not let the availability of a new model or dataset define the question. Start from the problem, then choose the instrument. The feeling of mastery over a tool is often a substitute for understanding the problem.

**Why this matters:** Fluency with a tool can hide confusion about the discipline.

The second idea comes from Works in Progress: a cop’s case for Flock, a license-plate reader system. The argument is startling because it comes from a police officer and it is not pro-surveillance. The line: people are right to want less police intrusion. The way to get there is more precision, not less.

The mechanism is substitution. If police have broad discretion to pull over vehicles or conduct random checks, that is intrusive and biased. If you give them a system that alerts only when a plate matches a stolen car or a known violent offender, you reduce the need for the dragnet. The technology is not neutral, but neither is the status quo it replaces.

The portable version: when you evaluate an intrusive technology or policy, ask what it replaces. The relevant comparison is not Flock versus nothing. It is Flock versus the broad, discretionary policing that exists now. The same move works in management, in hiring, in monitoring. A precise filter can be less intrusive than a vague but universally applied rule.

**Why this matters:** Too many debates are framed as more surveillance versus less, when the real axis is precision versus dragnet.

Both ideas make the same move: separate the discipline from the instrument, and the intervention from its alternative. That is a good trick to keep in the car.
