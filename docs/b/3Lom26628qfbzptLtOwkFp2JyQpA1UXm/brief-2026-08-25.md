## The World

### 1. A minimum-wage result that overturns a lunch-table assumption

Via Marginal Revolution, a new working paper by David Neumark [NOY-mark] and Emma Wohl [WOHL] asks a question most people think they already know the answer to: do minimum wages help workers in poor and low-income families? The answer, using the Survey of Income and Program Participation, is no — and not just no, but adverse.

The authors provide the first direct estimates of how minimum-wage increases affect low-wage workers in families at different points of the income-to-needs distribution. That matters because most minimum-wage research looks at workers in general, then assumes the benefits flow to the poor. This paper oversamples low-income families and follows the same workers over time. The result: minimum-wage increases reduce employment, hours, and earnings for initially employed low-wage workers in poor and low-income families. The effect does not show a clean gradient — it is not that the poorest are hit worst in a simple way — but it is broad enough to help explain why minimum wages do not reliably reduce poverty.

The mechanism is familiar to labor economists: employers adjust on margins other than the posted wage. But the framing is different. This is not a libertarian talking point. It is direct evidence about the exact group the policy is supposed to help.

**Why this matters:** If the goal is poverty reduction, the minimum wage may be a blunt instrument aimed at the wrong margin — and this paper gives you the direct evidence to say so.

### 2. The Iran sanctions campaign’s real pressure point is Beijing, not Tehran

Via Radio Farda [RAH-dee-oh FAR-dah], Persian-language coverage is framing the US sanctions campaign against Iran as a test that will be decided in China. The headline asks directly: if China does not cooperate, what then?

The underlying argument is that Washington’s leverage depends on Beijing’s willingness to enforce financial restrictions, shipping rules, and oil-purchase limits. Iran can endure a bilateral squeeze. It has done so for years. The harder question is whether China will absorb the cost of stricter enforcement. Beijing buys Iranian oil at a discount, extends credit, and sees Iran as a corridor in its Belt and Road infrastructure push. So the local read is not really about Iranian compliance. It is about Chinese incentives.

This is a triangular negotiation being covered in the West as a bilateral one. The Persian-language coverage spends less time on the State Department’s statements and more time on the practical question: which Chinese banks, ports, and insurers will blink first. If China continues to drag its feet, the sanctions architecture leaks. If China cooperates, the pressure on Tehran tightens sharply.

**Why this matters:** The actual decision-maker in this story may sit in Beijing, not Tehran — and the local coverage makes that explicit.

### 3. Vatican diplomacy goes back to Moscow

ZENIT [ZEN-it] and other outlets report that Pope Leo XIV is sending his foreign minister — effectively the Vatican’s top diplomat — to Moscow to engage with Russia and intercede on behalf of Ukraine.

This is a small story with a distinct shape. Most Western governments are still isolating Moscow. The Vatican is not a state with an army or an energy import bill, but it does have diplomatic reach, religious networks, and a long institutional memory. Sending a foreign minister rather than a lower-level envoy signals that Rome wants a real channel, not a photo opportunity.

There is no claim that this will produce a breakthrough. But it changes the diplomatic geometry. A neutral religious actor speaking to both Kyiv and Moscow creates room for humanitarian corridors, prisoner exchanges, or quiet negotiations that do not require either side to embrace the other publicly. The Vatican has done this before, often slowly, usually without resolution.

**Why this matters:** When a non-military, non-aligned state reopens a high-level channel to Moscow, it expands the menu of possible off-ramps — even if the odds remain long.

### 4. OpenAI disrupts a Russian influence campaign — and invites a harder question

OpenAI announced it banned a set of Russia-origin accounts that were using its models to promote a fake Israel-based think tank and a so-called sovereignty index that praised Russia and criticized the West.

The campaign itself is not novel. Influence operations have used fake experts, fake institutes, and synthetic indexes for years. What is new is the speed and polish. AI can generate plausible reports, multilingual commentary, and the appearance of institutional legitimacy at much lower cost than a human troll farm.

But there is a second layer here. The disclosure comes from OpenAI itself. That means the evidence is self-reported, and the narrative serves the company’s interest: it can say it is detecting and disrupting bad actors. The harder question is what other campaigns are undetected. The same models that power these fake think tanks also help ordinary users write memos, summarize emails, and brainstorm. The line between benign automation and coordinated manipulation is not always obvious from the output alone.

**Why this matters:** AI-assisted influence operations are now a platform-policing story, not just a spy story — and the only detective we are hearing from is the platform itself.

### 5. Data centers are the latest extraction industry in West Virginia

Literary Hub runs an essay by Matthew Neill Null that reframes the AI data-center buildout in Appalachia [ap-uh-LATCH-uh] as cultural erasure. Null writes about receiving ChatGPT-generated spam offering to market his novel — a novel about a dam that drowned communities in West Virginia — while data centers promise to consume land, water, and power in the same region.

The essay links the current tech boom to the older extraction playbook: coal, timber, dams, and now computation. The argument is not simply that data centers use resources. It is that the communities bearing the cost are often the same ones that have already been sacrificed to previous versions of American progress. Null calls it “ChatGPT slop” and lets the irony cut: the same technology that can imitate his voice about erasure is part of a new erasure.

This is not a standard NIMBY story about noise or property values. It is a cultural argument about what Appalachia is for. It connects the physical infrastructure of AI to the literary memory of a place that has repeatedly been told its future lies elsewhere.

**Why this matters:** The AI buildout has an environmental cost, but also a historical one — and this lens explains why local resistance may be deeper than tech companies expect.

## Paper of the Day

### AI help may feel like learning — and that is part of the problem

Today’s paper is “How AI Assistance Affects Human Skill Development: A Study of Learning with Logic Puzzles,” by Shang Wu, Catarina G. Belem [beh-LEM], Shuyuan Fu, Mark Steyvers [STY-vers], and Padhraic Smyth [smith], posted on arXiv [AR-kive].

The question is simple. AI assistance improves short-term task performance. We all know that. But what happens to your underlying skill when the AI is taken away? The researchers ran a controlled logic-puzzle experiment with three phases: before AI access, during AI access, and after AI access. Participants could request AI help on demand, and the researchers varied the cost of requesting that help. Lower-cost assistance led to more frequent AI use.

Here is the troubling result. Participants who requested AI assistance during the access phase performed worse after the assistance was removed than they did before. Worse at the same type of puzzle. The AI did not make them more capable. It made them more dependent, at least temporarily. And there was a second, sneakier finding: if you tried to predict their later unassisted performance from their AI-assisted performance, you would overestimate them. The AI-assisted scores looked like skill. They were not.

The authors used a Bayesian latent ability model to separate initial ability, post-AI ability, and participant-specific skill change. The crucial relationship: greater independent problem-solving effort during the AI-access phase was associated with larger gains in latent ability. In other words, the people who did the hard thing — struggled with the puzzle before asking for help — got better. The people who outsourced the struggle did not.

The mechanism is familiar to anyone who has taught. Skill develops when you work through the stuck point, not when you watch someone else solve it. AI assistance, when cheap and immediate, substitutes for exactly that struggle.

**Why this matters:** The short-term convenience of AI assistance may quietly undermine the skills you are trying to build — and the performance you see with AI in hand is not evidence that you learned.

## One Idea

### Durability is a filter, and early constraints can be protective

Today I want to give you two portable lenses. Both are about time.

The first comes from a LessWrong essay called “The Forkmakers.” Imagine our civilization fell tomorrow. A thousand years later, what would our descendants know about us? They would know surprisingly little about our greatest material triumphs. Reinforced concrete crumbles in a century. Asphalt dies faster. Hard drives last a few years. Acidic paper lasts decades. The ancient Mesopotamians wrote on clay tablets. We write on disposable storage.

But we do make things that last. Glass, ceramics, stainless steel. And the essay’s sharp observation is that the most durable, useful, widely distributed object we make is the fork. Our heirs, it suggests, would call us the Forkmakers.

This is a mental model, not a trivia item. Ask yourself: what is the fork equivalent of my work? What will survive after the quarterly metrics and the noise? The essay’s filter is not about being grand. It is about being durable. A fork is small, unglamorous, and everywhere. Most of what we produce will be forgotten. The question is whether anything survives that a future person would actually want to pick up and use.

Use this in conversation when someone is anxious about impact. Use it in your own research when choosing between a flashy project that will be obsolete in two years and a boring, reusable tool that might outlive you. You do not have to aim for monuments. Aim for forks.

The second lens comes from Nautilus. Britain’s postwar sugar rationing was an accidental experiment. Babies conceived or born during the rationing period grew into adults with measurably lower cancer risk. Decades later, the effects show up in aging, metabolism, and lifelong eating habits. Early sugar exposure — or its absence — left a lasting imprint.

This is not a diet tip. It is a reminder that constraints, especially early ones, can be protective in ways we do not feel at the time. The rationing generation did not feel lucky. But the absence of abundance was doing quiet work. The same logic applies to your attention, your schedule, your information diet. The friction you avoid today might be the protection you needed. Not all constraints are deprivation. Some are architecture.

**Why this matters:** Both ideas point the same direction — ease and abundance feel good in the moment, but durability and development often come from constraints.
