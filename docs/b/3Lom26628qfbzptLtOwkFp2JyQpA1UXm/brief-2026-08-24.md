### 1. The Sahel [sah-HEL] realignment, read from the inside

Through GDELT [GEE-delt] machine-translated sources, several West African outlets are covering the latest security pact shift in the Sahel [sah-HEL] as a procurement story, not an ideology story. The wire version emphasizes juntas moving further away from ECOWAS [eh-KOH-wahs] and toward new security partners. The local version spends more time on logistics contracts, fuel, border taxes, and which local commanders get new equipment. One recurring phrase in the translated pieces: sovereignty is not isolation; it is procurement. That reframing matters. If the realignment is really about who controls the security economy—escort routes, mining corridors, checkpoints—then the diplomatic map matters less than the village-level alliances that shift before the next rainy season. Those alliances determine who can move grain, cattle, and people. They also determine whether the state is a payer, a partner, or an obstacle. The local coverage keeps asking: who benefits? Not which country, but which faction, which trucking cooperative, which border town. That is the question the wire story often misses. **Why this matters:** The local framing inverts the standard Western read—a reminder of how much the story depends on the desk.

### 2. Export controls as industrial policy, not just national security

Non-US coverage of the new compute-control change is treating it as an industrial-policy story, not a Pentagon story. The US wire lede focuses on national security and which adversaries are denied access. The European and Asian ledes ask a different question: who gets to train the next wave of frontier models? Here is the argument they are making. When export controls restrict accelerators, they do not just block a sale. They redirect future demand toward domestic cloud providers and allied data centers. A firm that cannot buy the latest chips outright rents compute from a US or UK cloud. That rental income then funds the next buildout. Over time, the control becomes an input subsidy for the permitted few. The local coverage also notes that export controls tend to outlast the security crisis that justified them. Tariffs can be lifted in a trade deal; export controls become embedded in licensing regimes and procurement plans. So the real question is not whether the newest chip crosses a border today. It is whether the industrial capacity to train the next wave of models gets locked inside a small club. **Why this matters:** The local frame is about who gets to train the next wave of models, not about a press conference.

### 3. An essay on the exhaustion of the autofiction novel

The London Review of Books has a long essay arguing that the autofiction novel has calcified into mannerism. The writer is not saying autofiction was always bad. The point is that the form’s original trick—making the narrator’s unreliability feel dangerous—has become a tick. The current version is easy to parody: a writer-narrator, a residency, a confession of bad behavior, a self-aware wink, an ending that refuses to resolve. When every narrator performs the same shifty self-exposure, the first person stops being a window and becomes a brand. The essay’s sharpest claim is about what this does to readers. It gives them no place to stand outside the narrator’s performance, so even the most transgressive confessions start to feel safe. They are already accounted for by the genre. What might come next, the essay suggests, is fiction that borrows from bureaucratic documents, group chats, field notes, or outsourced narration—forms that force the writer to show a world outside the narrator’s head. **Why this matters:** A useful lens on why so much acclaimed new fiction feels the same—and what might come next.

### 4. AI’s political crisis becomes a policy puzzle

ChinaTalk’s new conversation with Anton Leicht [AHN-tohn LIGHT] offers a political-economy frame for the AI panic. The core argument is that even a small amount of AI-driven unemployment could upend politics if it hits junior white-collar workers. The reason is not just lost jobs. Entry-level lawyers, analysts, and policy staff are the pipeline for judgment. If agents replace the tasks that trained those people, institutions lose the next generation of decision-makers. Leicht’s policy response is deliberately non-calcifying. Do not impose blanket protections on occupations. Instead, subsidize junior hiring, collect better data on AI and labor, and fund the transition with corporate income taxes rather than token taxes. He also warns against giving the government AI equity or “golden shares”—it sounds like a way to capture surplus, but it makes the state both referee and shareholder. The data center backlash gets the same treatment: stop lecturing people about compute, and start offering local benefits like grid upgrades and training. On safety, the conversation rejects a pause but says a coordinated slowdown is possible. **Why this matters:** It turns AI politics from a vibe into a set of concrete levers—jobs, taxes, bandwidth, and trust.

### 5. Joint custody reforms shaped adult family formation

An economics paper covered by Marginal Revolution looks at the long shadow of family law. It uses the staggered adoption of joint-custody reforms across US states and 13 million American Community Survey observations. The finding: children exposed to joint-custody rules were about 7 percent less likely to become parents as adults, compared with similar children not exposed. The effect is symmetric for women and men, and it operates mostly through lower couple formation and more childlessness. That is not the usual story about money and fertility. It suggests that childhood institutions shape family preferences long before the question of affordability arrives. The authors also find that exposed individuals assortatively match—people with similar family histories partner with each other—so the effect is sub-additive inside couples. That is a subtle signal, but it points to preference formation, not just financial constraint. The policy implication is uncomfortable: a reform designed to help children after divorce may have changed how an entire cohort thought about partnership and parenthood. **Why this matters:** It reframes falling fertility as partly a childhood-institution story, not just a labor-market story.

Valor Econômico reports that the COP [cop] on desertification has reached the ministerial round already contaminated by geopolitics. It never gets a dramatic summit moment like a collapsing glacier, so it depends heavily on ministerial texts and funding formulas. If the text is watered down to avoid conflict, the money will follow the politics instead of the ecology. The Brazilian piece frames this as a familiar pattern: smaller environmental COPs become proxy arenas for larger trade and security fights. By the time the delegates leave, the dust is still there. **Why this matters:** When a summit about dust becomes a fight about geopolitics, the land is still degraded when the delegates fly home.

## Paper of the Day

### AI with Authority, from Application to Silicon

The paper of the day is from Jason Hickey, posted on arXiv [archive], and it is called “AI with Authority, from Application to Silicon.” The question it asks is simple and a little strange: what happens when the most expensive part of computing—formal verification—becomes cheap? For sixty years, machine-checked proof has been a luxury. You could prove a microprocessor correct if you had a team, a grant, and years. The standard assumption is that verification is a tax you pay after building something important. Hickey asked whether generative AI changes that price. His answer is in the title. At AI speed, verification is not just economical; it becomes essential. In five weeks, on consumer AI subscriptions, he directed a small fleet of AI agents from application code, through a verified compiler and an executive, to a RISC-V [risk-five] processor taped out on a community silicon shuttle. No human wrote the register-transfer-level code. No proof passed through human review. Let that sit for a second. One person, five weeks, consumer AI subscriptions, and a processor taped out. That is a completely different division of labor. How did that not turn into a hallucination disaster? The working discipline is called the Salt method. Mathematical claims travel between agents as kernel-checked artifacts. If a proof cannot pass the Lean [leen] 4 kernel, it does not travel. It does not matter how confident the model sounds. A hallucinated proof is not a risk to be managed; it is a paper that does not compile. Human attention is reserved for statements, designs, and rulings—the places where judgment actually matters. The paper publishes the full accounting: theorem provenance, a pre-registered token meter, floor-bounded human time, and an error ledger whose catch numbering runs to #256. That error ledger is the detail I keep thinking about. It means the process did not run flawlessly. It ran with a referee that caught mistakes. The so-what: the old assumption was that verification slows you down. The new assumption is that verification is what lets one person safely direct many agents. This matters beyond silicon—for code that moves money, for safety cases, for scientific pipelines. What changed is not that AI writes perfect code; it clearly does not. What changed is that being wrong can now be caught mechanically, link by link, at machine speed. The human stops being the writer of the proof and becomes the judge of the claim. **Why this matters:** The bottleneck in autonomous engineering may not be generation; it may be trust. If the referee becomes cheap, the bottleneck moves to asking the right question.

## One Idea

### Fast reasoning is not the same as learning

One idea today comes from Vladimir Nesov [VLAH-dee-meer NEH-sof], published on LessWrong. The title is “Twenty Years from RSI to Takeoff: Slow Learning, Scaling Slowdown, Industrial Explosion.” That is a forecast, but the useful part is not the specific year. It is a distinction: fast reasoning is not the same as fast learning. Current large language models are stunningly good at fast reasoning. They produce coherent text in milliseconds. But they learn slowly. They are trained in huge batches, then frozen. When you correct one, it does not update its weights. The model that made the mistake and the model you speak to next are the same model. That matters for recursive self-improvement, RSI [are-ess-eye]. The standard story says an AI that can rewrite its own code will immediately become better, and then the better version will rewrite again, and the curve goes vertical. Nesov’s point is that this story confuses reasoning with learning. If an AGI is fast at reasoning but slow at learning, improving itself is not a moment. It is a loop. And that loop runs through physical, industrial constraints—compute, chips, data, experiments, verification. He thinks we may get slow-learning AGIs around 2028 to 2032. They will be useful, but they will not set off an immediate software-only explosion because they do not have a fast learning loop. Then a compute buildout slowdown starts, and the next-model loop becomes expensive and slow. The wild part is the industrial explosion later, around 2050: the AI-directed economy builds out enough factories, energy, and data centers that the loop accelerates by a thousand times. At that point the speed of learning changes. The insight is not the specific year. It is the shape: a fast-reasoning system stuck in a slow-learning loop will produce a delay, not a detonation.

Now apply that to yourself. You also have a fast-reasoning mode and a slow-learning mode. You can talk fluently about a book you barely remember. That is fast reasoning. But if you close the loop—make a prediction, wait, get the result, update your note—you are learning. The best researchers do this on purpose. They do not just read more. They write down what they expect before running the experiment or reading the next paper. That tiny step turns comprehension into feedback. In conversation, use the same lens. When someone presents a polished argument, ask: what would change your mind? If they have no answer, they are in fast-reasoning mode. If they name a specific observation that would update them, they have a learning loop. One practical move: keep a prediction log. Before a meeting, write down what you think will be decided. After the meeting, compare. You will be wrong often. That is the point. The wrongness is data. Another move: when you finish a podcast or an article, do not summarize it. Generate the strongest objection before you open a note-taking app. That forces slow learning to begin while the reasoning is still warm. This is also why retrieval practice beats rereading. Testing yourself forces the system to generate an answer before seeing the correct one. It feels worse. That feeling is the signal that the loop is closing. Fluency feels good because it is fast reasoning; retrieval feels bad because it is slow learning. The tension is not a flaw to eliminate; it is the structure of improvement. **Why this matters:** The feeling of ease is a trap; fast output looks like learning, but only a closed loop—guess, feedback, update—actually changes you.
