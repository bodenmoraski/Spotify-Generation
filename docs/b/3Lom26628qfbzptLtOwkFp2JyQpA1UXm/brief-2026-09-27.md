## The World

### 1. Federal land as a property-rights failure

An economics argument is circulating this weekend, and it is aimed at one of the most quietly enormous facts in American life: the federal government owns about 473 million acres, roughly 21 percent of the lower forty-eight states. Marginal Revolution is drawing attention to a paper that reframes that land not as a conservation triumph, but as a property-rights failure. The paper walks through the privatization of federal lands up to 1891, when the withholding began. Its core claim: between 1870 and 1957, there was no demonstrable market failure or resource scarcity from private exploitation. Because land is nonmobile and observable, the authors argue, private property rights could have been assigned, with any externalities handled through Pigouvian restrictions or Coasean exchange. Instead, the land was locked into political and bureaucratic management under phrases like “multiple use” and “maximum sustained yield.” Those sound neutral, but the paper says they are a collective resource problem by another name — decisions made through politics rather than price signals.

**Why this matters:** If this argument is right, the Progressive Era consensus around federal land ownership was a costly policy choice, not an environmental necessity, and it still shapes fights over grazing, water, timber, and energy today.

### 2. Switzerland votes on a stricter kind of neutrality

Swiss voters are being asked to tighten the country’s rules on neutrality, and that could limit how closely Switzerland can coordinate with NATO [NAY-toh]. The referendum, covered in European security outlets, would push Swiss neutrality into a more restrictive legal box. For years, Switzerland has already sat outside NATO, but it has maintained quiet interoperability: participating in some exercises, sharing certain information, and allowing transit. A stricter neutrality rule would make that formal distance wider. It could restrict participation in military exercises, limit arms cooperation, and reduce the space for Switzerland to act as a diplomatic bridge. The timing is what makes this more than a local Swiss story. Europe is rearming, NATO is deepening its northern and eastern flanks, and neutral states are being squeezed between Russian pressure and Western security expectations.

**Why this matters:** If Switzerland votes to bind its own hands, it narrows the neutral corridor in Europe precisely when flexible neutrality might be most useful — and it signals that neutrality is becoming a domestic political identity rather than a foreign-policy tool.

### 3. Kyiv reads Beijing as the switch on Moscow’s war

A Ukrainian outlet, 24tv, is making an argument that does not get enough airtime in Western wire coverage: Russia’s war economy is now so dependent on Beijing [bay-jing] that China could, if it chose, cut off the material inputs that keep the war machine running. The piece argues that President Vladimir Putin’s dependency on Chinese supply chains, dual-use components, and payment rails has become the real constraint on Moscow’s options. In this framing, the battlefield is not just in Ukraine. It is also in every Chinese decision about what gets shipped, financed, and tolerated. That is a sharp reversal of the old dependency story, where Russia was the energy supplier and Europe was the customer.

**Why this matters:** If Kyiv’s read is correct, the future of the war depends less on any single front line and more on Beijing’s willingness to keep open the economic spigot — which means Western policy toward China is now inseparable from the war itself.

**Why this matters:** The meeting’s real product was not a communiqué; it was a signal to the rest of the world that the two powers can still talk — and Brazil’s coverage shows how that signal is being carried into the non-Western reading public.

## Paper of the Day

### PoEM [P-O-E-M]: predicting reinforcement learning without running it

Today’s paper asks a question that feels almost like a magic trick. Foundation models are post-trained with reinforcement learning to maximize rewards: human alignment, correctness, instruction following. That process is computationally expensive, sometimes unstable, and has to be run from scratch every time the reward model changes or you want to combine multiple rewards. So the authors ask: given a new reward function, can you predict what reinforcement learning would do without actually running reinforcement learning?

The answer is yes, with a framework called PoEM, posted to the arXiv [archive]. The story starts with a mathematical observation. If the new reward function can be written as a linear combination of existing rewards, then the new policy in log-space can also be written as a linear combination of the existing log-policies. That is elegant but maybe not surprising. The surprising part is what they found next: even when the rewards are not linearly connected, the log-policies from RL training often span an approximately low-rank subspace across rewards. In plain terms, the many possible post-trained models live in a much smaller space than you would expect. That means you can use a set of already-trained models as a basis, estimate the weighting coefficients from the rewards or basis policy outputs on samples, and assemble the new policy without a new training run.

The so-what is substantial. It reframes the cost of alignment. Instead of paying for a full RL run every time a reward changes, you might store a basis set of already aligned policies and interpolate. That makes it much cheaper to explore many reward formulations, which is exactly what safety research needs — because safety is often about iterating on rewards, and cheaper iteration means more iteration. But the low-rank property also raises a caution. If post-trained policies live in a low-dimensional subspace, then predicting sensitive policy outputs may be easier for adversaries, too. The paper does not solve safety. It changes the economics of asking safety questions, and that is a genuine contribution.

**Why this matters:** The cost of alignment has long been the hidden tax on every safety experiment; PoEM suggests that tax might be sharply lower, while also revealing a structural property worth watching.

## One Idea

### Addictions are anesthesia

A LessWrong essay by Chris Lakin makes a reframe that could change how you treat your own bad habits. The title is “Addictions are anesthesia.” The core claim: most people misunderstand addictions. They think they are addicted “to” scrolling, vaping, overworking, or doomscrolling. Lakin argues they are actually addicted to the strategy, not the substance or behavior. The addiction functions as anesthesia, a way of managing suffering. If you are in pain, it is often wise to employ some method of anesthesia. That means if you forcibly remove the anesthesia while the pain remains, one of two things happens: you get overwhelmed, or you find another way to numb.

The mechanism matters because it explains failure. Someone struggles to quit scrolling, succeeds for a week, then picks up overworking or drinking. That is not weak willpower. That is addiction whack-a-mole. The underlying pain has not gone anywhere. The behavior was a solution to a problem, and the problem is still there.

How to use this. In your own life, when you notice a habit you despise, do not ask, “How do I stop this?” Ask, “What is this protecting me from feeling?” That question is uncomfortable, but it points at the actual lever. In conversation, if someone says they cannot stop overworking, do not suggest a time-management system. Ask what would happen if they sat still for an hour with no task. The answer usually names the suffering directly: loneliness, fear of failure, unresolved conflict, or grief. In research or product work, the same lens applies to user behavior. When users doomscroll, the immediate problem is not just the app’s design. It is the pain the scroll numbs. You can design for reduced suffering rather than just added friction.

The deeper point is even more useful: addictions are a measure of suffering and internal conflict. The worse the habit, the bigger the load it is carrying. That turns habit failure from a moral failing into diagnostic data.

### Why we stay afraid

A second piece, from Nautilus, supplies a mechanism for why this suffering can linger. Researchers found a brain region in mice that explains how fear persists after threats fade. The old puzzle was why anxiety outlasts the danger. The new answer points to a circuit that acts as a residual alarm, not a failure of conscious thought or willpower. This matters because it connects directly to the addiction model. If fear is stored at the circuit level, then trying to think your way out of it is like arguing with a smoke detector. The lever is to change the situation or internal state that keeps the alarm fed. Together, these two ideas form a coherent upgrade: suffering is physiological and strategic. The behavior you hate is often the anesthesia for it. Go after the pain, not the painkiller.

**Why this matters:** The feeling that a bad habit is a character flaw is itself part of the anesthesia; seeing it as a strategy for pain gives you a better place to aim.
