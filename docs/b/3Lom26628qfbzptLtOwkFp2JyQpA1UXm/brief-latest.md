## The World

### 1. The Gilded Age’s bottom ninety percent

The economist Tyler Cowen, writing at Marginal Revolution, flags a new history by Vincent Geloso called *The Great Enrichment*. The subtitle is a reframe in itself: *A New History of American Growth and Inequality, 1865–1945*. The argument is deliberately provocative. From 1870 to the start of World War One, America experienced the fastest improvement in quality of life for the poorest ninety percent of Americans ever seen in human history up to that point. Not just wages. Food variety, clothing, sanitation, mortality, the ability to move toward a better job. Geloso’s second claim is sharper: those bottom-ninety gains were faster than gains for the top ten percent and as fast as gains for the top one percent. He calls the period a lean, mean egalitarian growth machine. The engine was not redistribution or charity; it was a fluid market economy that let people leave failing places and take better opportunities.

Why this matters: If the standard story treats the Gilded Age as an inequality disaster, this reframe says the era raised the floor faster than the chandelier—and we should be precise about which mechanism did the raising before we dismantle it.

### 2. Non-Anglophone coverage of Ukraine’s frozen diplomacy

Via GDELT [GEE-delt], the machine-translated French outlet *L’Humanité* [loo-mah-nee-TAY] interviews the French diplomat Jean de Gliniasty [zhahn duh glee-nyah-stee]. His claim is blunt: Europe has no diplomatic objective in Ukraine, and the Russians do not really want to negotiate. He is not saying this from a Brussels panel as a careful consensus line. It is a left-wing French paper arguing something harder: the continent has no end-state, so the war has become a managed condition rather than a problem with a solution. Western wire services would likely frame this as “European leaders debate peace terms.” The local framing is darker. Without a defined objective, negotiation is theater. Russia uses the pause to regroup. Europe uses the frozen conflict to avoid admitting it has no plan. Both sides, in his reading, benefit from the stalemate’s continuance.

Why this matters: If both sides are managing the stalemate rather than seeking an exit, then peace feelers should be read as tactical noise, not as signals of settlement.

### 3. The statistic too large to feel: children and armed conflict

A new count says 260 million children in Africa lived near armed conflict in 2025. That is not a typo. The figure runs through African media outlets, including Ahram Online via African Media Digest. Separately, a *Modern Diplomacy* report argues that women and girls in Tigray [TIG-ray] bear the brunt of violence, famine, and fragility. The temptation is to treat this as a humanitarian headline and move on. The stronger argument is that it is a development failure unfolding before the next generation reaches adulthood. Schooling is interrupted. Vaccination campaigns miss windows. Labor markets absorb children early. Marriage timing shifts. A quarter-billion children are being shaped by proximity to violence, and that shaping will show up in economies, migration flows, and political stability fifteen years from now. Tigray is not an isolated tragedy; it is one node in a wider belt where human capital is being spent before it is formed.

Why this matters: When a number gets this large, it stops provoking action and starts producing numbness—so the real story is not the count, but which institutions are already planning for a lost cohort.

### 4. Narratives that kill: ICE, emergency rooms, and medical racism

In early November, ICE agents brought someone in their custody to Adventist Health White Memorial Medical Center in Boyle Heights, Los Angeles—a neighborhood more than ninety percent Hispanic and Latino. The hospital says it provided dignified care. Activists say that when agents were not given free rein, they retaliated. One fact is not disputed: federal agents formed a line across the ambulance entrance. For about two hours, ambulances were diverted. Steven W. Thrasher’s essay at *Literary Hub* argues this is not just a policing incident. It is a collision of two narratives. The narrative that migrants are threats legitimizes immigration enforcement inside a place of care. The narrative of medical racism determines who gets the benefit of the doubt and who is seen as dangerous. Together, they turn an emergency room into a border checkpoint.

Why this matters: The damage is not only symbolic. It is measured in diverted ambulances and delayed care—narratives have logistics.

### 5. The ugly phone booth and the wrong question

Patrick Collison recently asked why so many public objects—phone booths, water fountains—used to be beautiful and are now ugly. He wondered whether the ugliness reflects some metaphysical incoherence in modern Western culture. A response on LessWrong, by someone writing as “jenn,” says no: the question is being asked at the wrong level. The essay pushes past nostalgia. The old red phone box was maintained under a public monopoly with design standards, replacement-part budgets, and a civic vision. The new black utilitarian one survives procurement review, vandalism risk, and liability lawyers. Ugliness is a systems outcome, not a mood. The author points to pieces like “Eight architecture myths BUSTED” to argue that romantic decline stories miss the actual constraints: maintenance costs, safety codes, risk transfer, and standardized purchasing.

Why this matters: If you diagnose ugliness as cultural decay, you call for a revival. If you see it as procurement incentives, you change the maintenance contract or the risk manual—and public spaces might actually improve.

## Paper of the Day

### Clouds of Credit: Mapping the Financial Web Behind AI Compute

The paper comes from the National Bureau of Economic Research, by Stijn Van Nieuwerburgh [stine van NEW-er-burg] and Zijin Su [ZEE-jin SOO]. The question is simple to ask and very hard to answer: who is actually funding the AI buildout, and what happens if the revenue does not arrive?

Imagine a data center campus. A chipmaker sells the processors. A cloud provider leases compute by the hour. A lender finances a project entity. An equity investor puts in a cushion. A power utility promises capacity. Now repeat this thousands of times, across firms, borders, and contracts. The authors map that web. Their first finding is reassuring in the near term: by mid-2026, substantial equity cushions protect creditors against the very large obligations already outstanding. But the map also shows concentrated exposures and interconnected claims. Weak links. Debt finances nearly sixty percent of the expansion, and much of it is non-recourse borrowing. That means project lenders cannot chase the parent company; they are exposed to the project’s cash flows, not the sponsor’s balance sheet.

Then the paper runs a calibrated model. In a low-productivity state, losses reach sixteen point eight trillion dollars across shareholders and creditors. The average loss-given-default on debt is forty percent. That is a tail scenario, not a forecast. The more surprising result: suppressing contractual payment spillovers among key firms reduces private losses by roughly thirty percent. A huge share of the damage is network propagation, not first-round default.

The so-what: this is a financial stability paper wearing an AI costume. The buildout may be socially excessive because each firm does not internalize how its expansion reduces competitors’ revenues. The financial web has cushions, but its weak links are the kind that amplify a slowdown into a cascade. If you are doing research, the map is the method—do not ask whether a firm is safe; ask who must pay if its next revenue state fails.

Why this matters: The AI story is also a credit story, and the tail risk is measured in the tens of trillions—not because the chips fail, but because the contracts connect.

## One Idea

### The useful question inside a family myth

The essayist Jordan Lee Borgman writes in *Aeon* [EE-on] about families who said their grandmother was a seal. In Irish and Scottish coastal communities, selkie [SEL-key] stories were not just bedtime tales; they were genealogies. The selkie sheds its sealskin, lives as a human, then finds the skin and returns to the sea. Borgman’s essay argues these stories are not whimsy. They are a record of grief: grief for kinship with the non-human world, grief for the moment of separation, grief for violence done to creatures we once called family.

Here is the mechanism. A story can preserve what a census cannot. The selkie’s stolen skin is a precise symbol of a broken bond. When the seal returns to the sea, the family is left with loss and a half-human child. That is not a misunderstanding of biology. It is a way of saying: our prosperity came at the cost of a relationship. The story is a grief protocol. It tells the next generation what was lost, and that the loss matters.

Now the application. In conversation, when someone says something like “my grandmother was a seal,” do not smile and move on. Ask what loss the story is keeping alive. In your own research, treat local lore as an archive. It may encode environmental changes, extinctions, or migration histories that formal records miss. The portable idea is this: for any myth that sounds impossible, replace the question “is it true?” with “what break is it mourning?” That shift moves you from correction to comprehension.

A shorter companion idea comes from *Nautilus* [NAW-tih-lus]. The essay “Why Are These Polar Bears Thriving?” by Elena Kazamia investigates a paradox: in parts of northern Canada, some polar bear populations are doing better than the dominant climate narrative predicts. The point is not denial. It is that “thriving” is uneven. Local sea ice dynamics, prey availability, and movement corridors matter more than the global average. The mental model: exceptions are not noise; they are the control group. When a species, a market, or a person defies a predicted decline, do not use the exception to dismiss the trend. Use it to identify the mechanism. Ask: what specific condition made this case different? That question moves a conversation from ideology to mechanics.

Why this matters: Both ideas protect you from the two most common failure modes—believing a story is just a story, and believing an exception is just an excuse.
