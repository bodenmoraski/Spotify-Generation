## The World

### 1. China’s Starlink response is an AI infrastructure story wearing a rocket shell

Via ChinaTalk, Paddy Stephens reports on China’s attempt to match SpaceX in low-Earth orbit. In July, the first stage of the Long March 10B slowed itself into a netted metal box on a sea barge, making the China Aerospace Science and Technology Corporation, or CASC [C-A-S-C], only the third entity after SpaceX and Blue Origin to recover a rocket booster. Then in December, Zhuque-3 [ZHOO-chuh three], a reusable rocket built by the commercial firm LandSpace, ended its first attempt as a fireball in the Gobi Desert.

The Western headline is about reusable rockets, but the real story is constellation capacity. China is racing to build its own Starlink-class networks, using both state-owned CASC and commercial firms like LandSpace. That matters for AI because low-Earth orbit satellites are not just internet plumbing. They are the last-mile supply chain for data: drone swarms, smart factories, edge compute, and battlefield sensing all need low-latency connections that do not depend on foreign undersea cables or foreign satellites. Reusable rockets lower the cost of putting that infrastructure up. So the rocket catch is not a space spectacle. It is a signal that China intends to own the routing layer under its AI deployments, from training data transport to real-time inference in places where fiber cannot go.

**Why this matters:** The next AI bottleneck may not be chips or data centers; it may be who can move data through orbit cheaply and under their own control.

### 2. ECOWAS Court and women’s rights in West Africa: the gap between ruling and reality

A survey of the ECOWAS [EH-koh-wahs] Court’s record asks what is actually changing for women in West Africa. ECOWAS is the Economic Community of West African States, and its regional court has become a venue for rights litigation when national courts stall. The article, via The Conversation, argues that the court has delivered important rulings on inheritance, divorce, and protection from harmful practices. But the record is uneven. A judgment in Abuja does not automatically become enforcement in Accra or Freetown.

The local frame is often missed by outside coverage. This is not a story about a court issuing noble declarations. It is about who benefits when a regional body can rule against a member state, and who still waits. Women’s groups have learned to use the court strategically, because it can create legal precedent even when a national legislature will not act. But implementation depends on domestic judges and ministries, and some states simply ignore rulings. The result is a two-level system: rights move faster at the regional level, but lived outcomes still depend on the local official.

**Why this matters:** Regional courts are doing the work national governments avoid, but their influence stops exactly where enforcement begins—and that tells you more than the rulings themselves.

### 3. The Hyphen Unites: Arab-Jewish identity as an argument, not just a memoir

Public Books has a conversation with the historian Avi Shlaim [AH-vee SHLAH-eem], one of the most influential scholars of Israel and the Arab world. Shlaim is not new to this conversation, but the timing matters. As mainstream Israeli politics hardens around ethno-nationalist definitions of belonging, Shlaim argues that the Arab-Jewish hyphen should unite, not divide. He speaks from his own history: a Jewish boy born in Baghdad, shaped by Arab and Jewish worlds, who refuses to treat those identities as opposites.

The cultural argument is sharper than a memoir. Shlaim is challenging a founding narrative that says those identities were always separate and always hostile. He points to class and caste differences inside Israeli politics, and to how destructive ethno-nationalist ideologies flatten a much more complicated Mediterranean and Middle Eastern history. The hyphen, in his telling, is not nostalgia. It is a political claim: there was, and still is, a way to be both.

**Why this matters:** At a moment when the public debate over Israel and Palestine is often binary, Shlaim gives language for a third position—and that language has real contestational force.

### 4. Hostage diplomacy for everyday negotiation: patience works, but the piece is thin

A Kellogg Insight piece asks what hostage diplomacy can teach us about everyday negotiation. The headline is promising: negotiators in hostage cases operate under extreme time pressure, high stakes, and asymmetric information. The lessons are mostly familiar—slow down, understand constraints, build relationships, separate the person from the problem. The article does not offer many specifics, so I’ll keep this one short. But the useful kernel is genuine: in a crisis, the urge to close quickly often produces worse deals than the discipline of staying with the process. That applies to a salary negotiation or a stuck project as much as to a kidnap case. The difference is the stakes, not the mechanism.

**Why this matters:** The negotiation mistake most of us make—rushing to a yes because the pressure feels unbearable—is the same mistake hostage negotiators train against, and we can borrow their patience without the drama.

### 5. Beyond MLK: the women reporters who made the March on Washington

Literary Hub runs an excerpt from Marcia Chatelain’s work on the women behind the 1963 March on Washington. The story focuses on Lula Patterson, a reporter for the Baltimore Afro-American, who was assigned a puff piece about how the women looked, how they dressed, and what hats they wore. She decided the march was not a fashion event. Through the crowds, she saw a political event, and she covered it as one.

Chatelain’s point is not just that women were present. It is that Black women reporters were building the archive of the movement even when their editors wanted society pages. They wrote with context, with political judgment, and with an eye for the ordinary details that later histories often erase. That matters because the cast of a familiar history changes when you look at who was taking notes. The march did not happen only on the podium. It happened in the notebooks of women who understood that a color story about hats would be a lie.

**Why this matters:** Recovering the women reporters is not additive trivia; it changes what we think the event was, because the archive itself was shaped by their choices.

## Paper of the Day

### An LLM is the new research assistant—and it found 3,460 replication problems

The question was not whether a large language model could write an economics paper. It was whether an LLM could check one.

A new paper by Matthew Schwartz, Isaiah Andrews [eye-ZAY-uh AN-drooz], and Jesse Shapiro [JES-ee shuh-PEER-oh] asks something practical: give a model the published replication package from an economics article, and see whether it can reproduce the original calculations, flag discrepancies, improve the code, and extend the analysis. Across 4,452 published replication packages from five economics journals, the workflow flagged discrepancies in 3,460 articles or their appendices. That is not a small anomaly. It is a big audit.

The story is not about a model replacing a researcher. It is about turning replication into a routine, scalable act rather than a heroic one. The workflow first tries to reproduce the original numbers. Then it runs automated sensitivity checks. Then it asks whether the same calculation could be done faster or better. In 496 articles, the workflow reduced computation time by more than a factor of ten at similar or greater accuracy. In 923 articles, it produced an extension that the original authors did not include but that fit the paper’s own goals.

The so-what is double. First, if an LLM can flag discrepancies in three thousand papers, then the scarcity is no longer the ability to check. The scarcity is the will to check and the institutions that reward checking. Second, the workflow does not just police old work; it produces new work. An extension that follows from a paper’s own assumptions is not a gimmick. It is the beginning of a different research loop, where the critique and the next paper arrive together.

Cowen’s line is that this paper may just fade into the background. But that would be the wrong end. The tool matters because it lowers the cost of distrust.

**Why this matters:** When verification becomes cheap, the real question is no longer which papers fail; it is why so many fields choose not to look.

## One Idea

### Reason is more than a tool

Two pieces this week push against a habit we have built into how we talk about intelligence: treating it as optimization.

Sasha Mudd’s Aeon essay argues that if intelligence is just optimization—given a goal, find the best path—then machines will outrun us. They already do, in many narrow domains. But Kant’s point, Mudd says, is that human reason is not just a tool for satisfying whatever inclination happens to be live. Reason can step back and ask whether the goal itself is the right one. A machine optimizes inside a preference function. A human can revise the preference function. That is not mysticism. It is the difference between calculating well and judging well.

The practical version is easy to miss because optimization feels like thinking. When you are tuning a schedule, refining a prompt, or choosing the best model for a task, the mental motion is real. But if the objective is inherited—more output, faster reading, higher engagement—then you are not reasoning about ends; you are computing means. The Aeon essay is a reminder that the most important intellectual move is often the one before optimization: asking what the metric is for.

So how do you use this? In conversation, when someone says “I’m just being logical,” ask what end their logic is serving. The chain of inference may be flawless, but the first premise may be borrowed. In your own research, notice when you choose the easily computable measure because it is easy. That is optimization choosing your question for you. Stop and ask whether the metric captures the phenomenon you actually care about. In self-improvement, do not just optimize the calendar. Once a week, ask whether the calendar optimizes the right life. That sounds grand, but it is a concrete habit: block thirty minutes to question the week’s goal, not just its task list.

The second piece adds a warning. In Nautilus, Dariusz Jemielniak [DAHR-yoosh yem-YEL-nyak] writes that when an AI says “I am just a machine, don’t trust me,” that confession is itself a mechanical trick. It feels like authenticity, but it is another trained output. The lesson: be suspicious of both optimization and of confessions that sound like stepping outside optimization. “I’m just being rational” and “I’m just a machine” can both be moves in a game.

**Why this matters:** The feeling of efficiency is not the same as the exercise of judgment—and fluency, in tools or in people, can quietly replace the harder work of asking what the goal should be.
