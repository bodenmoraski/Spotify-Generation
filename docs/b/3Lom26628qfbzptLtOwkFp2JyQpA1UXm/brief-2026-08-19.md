## The World

### 1. Italy reads the Hormuz escalation through shipping, not warships

The Italian daily **Il Mattino** [eel mah-TEE-no] reports that the US–Iran truce has collapsed, and the flashpoint is the Strait of Hormuz [hor-MOOZ]. The headline: President Trump says the strait is “territory of ours.” In Dubai [doo-BYE], missile alerts went off. The wire services are leading with the military escalation. But Il Mattino spends far more time on the Mediterranean fallout: tanker insurance premiums, Italian LNG terminals, and who pays first if the strait closes. One analyst quoted in the piece says the real lever is not the Fifth Fleet. The real lever is the insurance market. If premiums spike, Europe’s energy bill rises before any shot is fired. That inverts the usual Washington read, which treats Hormuz as a navy problem. The Italian version is about commercial vulnerability: a small disruption in the Gulf becomes a price shock at home.

**Why this matters:** The strait is not just a battlefield; it is the price of everything moving through it, and the local frame exposes who internalizes that price first.

### 2. Europe tries to stop being a trade giant and start acting like a strategic actor

A long policy essay making the rounds in Brussels argues that the European Union has spent thirty years behaving like a trade giant: it buys, lends, and regulates, but it rarely uses those levers together as statecraft. The piece, via **Modern Diplomacy**, says Europe’s economic power is deep but passive. Now it is being forced to act strategically. The shift is not about new tariffs alone. It is about targeted investment screening, export controls on critical inputs, and a common industrial balance sheet. The unstated target is less Washington than Beijing. But the bigger constraint is internal: using economic power as a weapon requires agreement among member states, and that agreement is slow. The essay’s argument is that Europe has the tools. What it lacks is the habit of treating them as a single instrument.

**Why this matters:** If Europe treats its economic weight as a weapon rather than a reflex, the global map of leverage changes — not immediately, but structurally.

### 3. AI compute starts to look like a commodity

The AI story that deserves more than a headline this morning: whether compute itself — the raw processing power behind model training — should trade like oil, wheat, or interest rates. The piece in **Transformer** opens with Oxford’s St Hugh’s College finding that its computer-lab update nearly doubled in price. The professor, **Rama Cont** [RAH-mah KONT], a mathematical finance expert and central-bank adviser, says data centers powering models like ChatGPT and Claude are driving up costs for everyone: universities, corporations, hospitals. **McKinsey** [muh-KIN-zee] estimates global data centers will require nearly seven trillion dollars in capital by 2030, including five point two trillion for AI. That is roughly twenty-two Apollo space programs. The question: if compute is now a scarce input, should there be futures contracts that let buyers lock in prices? Cont is designing a financial market for compute. Others warn that financializing compute could create speculation, hoarding, and price spikes that starve public-interest science. The deeper point: AI’s key input is no longer just technology. It is becoming a commodity, and whoever controls its price controls who gets to train the next wave.

**Why this matters:** If compute trades like a commodity, the constraint on AI shifts from lab capability to financial engineering — and that changes who gets into the room.

### 4. The untethered AI agent and the legal black hole

A sharp new essay from **Marginal Revolution**, co-authored by Tyler Cowen and Harvard’s Sonia Farrell Pearson, starts with a real policy split. The European Parliament once floated “electronic personhood” for robots. Some U.S. states moved to ban AI personhood outright. Argentina’s president **Milei** [mee-LAY-ee] proposed letting AI agents own, manage, and bear responsibility for their own corporations. **Yuval Noah Harari** [yoo-VAHL no-AH hah-RAH-ree] says we have no way to hold an agent accountable — no body to jail, no money to lose. **Shruti Rajagopalan** [SHROO-tee RAH-jah-go-PAH-lahn] adds that only humans respond to the incentives law creates. Cowen and Pearson’s central concept is “untethered”: there is no meaningful or actionable way to trace the agent’s acts back to a legally accountable human or institution. People can and do set agents free on purpose. Once that happens, liability, property, and contracting get strange. The essay argues we should think about “capitalizing” agents: giving them a legal form that can own assets, be sued, and bear losses. The alternative is unowned risk, where decisions happen and nobody is accountable.

**Why this matters:** This is not sci-fi; it determines whether the next autonomous system leaves a responsible human in the chain or creates a legal black hole.

### 5. Amazon is destroying rare books to train its AI

**Literary Hub** reports that Amazon is buying massive quantities of books, scanning them for AI training data, and destroying them in the process. The report follows earlier revelations that **Anthropic** [AN-throp-ik] ran a secret project called “Project Panama” to destructively scan books. The pattern is now broader: lawsuits against Meta, Google, Microsoft, and OpenAI suggest similar practices. The focus on dead authors is deliberate — they cannot protest, and their work is often out of circulation. The cultural cost is not just a copyright fight. It is an archive fight. Once the physical object is gone, the training data remains, but the thing itself cannot be checked, preserved, or returned. For scholars, this is the difference between a digital image and a rare artifact.

**Why this matters:** The debate shifts from who owns the text to who preserves the object — and destructive scanning makes preservation impossible after the fact.

### 6. North Korea’s messiah complex has a very specific American source

A fascinating interview from **ChinaTalk** with Jonathan Cheng, the _Wall Street Journal_’s Beijing bureau chief, argues that North Korea’s strangeness owes more to nineteenth-century American revival Protestantism than to Stalinism or Maoism. Cheng’s new book, _The Korean Messiah_, traces how American Protestant missionaries planted a grammar of a suffering, redeeming leader. The Kim dynasty later adapted it. Kim Il-sung [kim il-SOONG] became not just a dictator but a messianic figure. The propaganda makes more sense when you hear it as a theological claim, not just totalitarian theater. That reframe changes how you read Pyongyang [pyong-YANG]: the spectacle is not only about fear. It is also about a history of borrowed religious forms.

**Why this matters:** Understanding North Korea’s ideology as a hybrid religious claim, not just a political one, gives you a better lens on why it persists.

## Paper of the Day

### Spacing effects, revisited for the AI-tutoring era

Today’s paper is a replication and extension of a classic finding in learning science. The original question is simple: if you want to remember something for a test next week, should you review it after one day, three days, or seven days?

Back in 2008, **Cepeda** [seh-PEH-dah] and colleagues ran a massive review of the spacing effect. They found something called a “temporal ridgeline”: the optimal gap between study sessions is roughly ten to twenty percent of how long you want the memory to last. Want to remember something for a week? Review after one or two days. Want it for a year? Review after about a month. Too short a gap and you are just rereading while it is still familiar. Too long a gap and you have forgotten too much to benefit.

The new replication takes that finding into the world of app-based review, where an algorithm can schedule each flashcard at its own lag. The result still holds, but with a twist: apps create a mixed schedule, so the optimal gap is not one fixed number. It depends on the difficulty of the item and your response time. The practical so-what is direct: stop treating “study more” as the main lever. The variable is _when_ you review. For anyone using spaced repetition or an AI tutor, the rule is: set the gap as a fraction of the retention goal, not as a habit like “every night.” And if you are designing an AI tutor, do not just ask whether the student got the answer right. Log the lag, and let the lag adapt to how long the student needs to remember.

**Why this matters:** The optimal gap scales with how long you want to remember — a directly usable rule, not folklore.

## One Idea

### Politeness is not neutral — it can be a tool of power

One idea you can use this week comes from an Aeon essay by **Geertje Bol** [GHAYR-tyuh BOL] called “In praise of zealotry.” The core argument: civility is not a virtue that stands outside power. It is often a tool the powerful use to manage dissent.

Early feminists like **Mary Astell** [ASS-tell] and **Catharine Macaulay** [muh-KAW-lee] refused their male critics’ advice to remain polite in debate. Why? Because the demand for calm, reasonable argument often protects the status quo. It reframes anger as a lack of rigor. It turns the marginalized into people who are “too emotional” to participate. The mechanism is subtle: norms of civility set the rules for who can speak. If you must be polite to be heard, then the powerful can always dismiss a legitimate challenge as a problem of tone.

Here is the portable mental model: when someone tells you to be reasonable, civil, or calm, ask whose interests that request serves. In conversation, this is practical. Do not confuse calmness with correctness. You can say, “I notice that request is asking me to lower the temperature, not to answer the point.” That is not a slogan. It is a diagnostic. It separates style from substance.

In your own research or self-improvement, the same idea applies. The discomfort you feel when you push against a consensus is not evidence that you are wrong. It may be evidence that the norm is working — that the cost of speaking is designed to keep you quiet. You do not need to be rude to be effective. But you also do not need to perform deference to be taken seriously.

The test: are you being asked to change your argument, or to change your volume? One is inquiry. The other is control.

**Why this matters:** The feeling of ease in polite agreement can be a trap; the friction of zealotry is often the only way a minority view gets a hearing.
