## The World

### 1. In Korea, a direct line leaves Seoul on hold

Start with the Korean peninsula, because here the title is the story. Washington is courting Pyongyang [pyawng-YAHNG] directly, and the North is raising its price in response. Seoul [sohl] is watching from the outside of the room. The wire piece itself is thin on terms — no agreed framework, no announced date — but the strategic signal is not in the details. It is in the structure: if Kim Jong Un [kim jawng uhn] can negotiate with Washington without going through Seoul, the North Korean playbook changes. It no longer needs to engage the South as a counterpart; it needs the South as a spectator.

The report comes through an NPR affiliate feed, not a deeply reported exclusive, but its value is that it names a fear most summit coverage leaves implicit: an ally can lose the table without losing the treaty.

**Why this matters:** Alliances are built on consultation, and the perception of being sidelined can be as corrosive as an actual exclusion. Seoul’s next move is not to complain in public; it is to force itself back into the agenda.

### 2. Indonesia: the scale of the place versus confidence in its institutions

Irene Zhang went to Jakarta [juh-KAR-tuh] in July, and the essay opens with a friend’s warning over durian [DOOR-ee-ahn]: “Indonesia will be a military dictatorship soon. It’s so over for Southeast Asian democracy.” The next morning, Zhang walks into Grand Indonesia, a glitzy central mall, and puts her backpack through a baggage scanner. Security guards with metallic badges flash past. The essay is not an alarm; it is an observation about scale.

We often talk about Indonesia as a stable democratic success story — the third-largest democracy, a Muslim-majority anchor. Zhang’s notes do not deny that. They show the surface tension. The sheer size of the archipelago, the visible security apparatus even in a luxury shopping center, the casual fatalism of a neighboring observer — these are not evidence of an imminent coup. They are reasons to ask whether Indonesian stability is institutional, or just enormous and hard to move.

**Why this matters:** The standard story about Indonesia is an investor narrative. Zhang’s on-the-ground notes suggest the story may be scale rather than resilience, and that changes what you watch for — not votes alone, but the machinery around ordinary spaces.

### 3. Tariffs did not crash imports; the AI boom hid the damage

The United States raised tariffs in 2025 to levels not seen since the Great Depression. The predicted result was a collapse in imports. It did not happen. Imports went up. A new working paper from the National Bureau of Economic Research tries to account for that missing collapse, and the answer is uncomfortable for both sides of the tariff debate.

The authors build an open-economy model with tariff heterogeneity, inventories, and a shock that captures the AI-driven investment boom. Their decomposition says: absent the investment boom, imports would have fallen by ten percent and activity would have contracted by seven-tenths of a percent. The tariffs still did damage — just not in the expected place. Because the 2025 increases landed mostly on consumption goods and intermediate inputs, they acted like a supply shock. Costs rose, and inflation got worse. But capital goods were relatively spared, so the AI buildout could continue. The boom masked the output hit while amplifying the price hit.

**Why this matters:** This is the economic version of the map behind the map. If you read import volumes alone, you think tariffs did nothing. If you read inflation alone, you think they did everything. The truth is both — and AI is not a side note; it is the variable that rewrote the baseline.

### 4. Guns flow south; the violence that sends people north is partly made in America

Ieva Jusionyte [YEH-vah yoo-shon-YEE-teh] is an anthropologist who studies borders as sites of state violence. Her work follows American guns south into Mexico, and the title of the interview is blunt: “Our Guns Make the Violence That People Are Fleeing.” In the conversation with Public Books, she says she was already at the border, already an ethnographer, and then she decided to follow the guns south.

The reframe is simple. We usually talk about migration as a border security problem. Jusionyte argues that the violence people flee is not just Mexican criminality; it is armed by a legal U.S. market. If American gun stores are the source of weapons that make communities unlivable, then the border crisis is a downstream consequence of domestic commerce, not only foreign disorder.

**Why this matters:** It relocates responsibility from the fence to the factory. That is a difficult sentence to answer at a dinner table, but it is also a direct link between two policies usually debated in different rooms.

### 5. The AI cure for cancer is still a gap, not a pipeline

Dario Amodei [DAH-ree-oh AH-moh-day] of Anthropic [an-THROP-ik] said this week that saying AI will cure cancer is now more a cliche than an inspiration, and most people find it deceptive. Sam Altman has floated that with ten gigawatts of compute, AI might figure out how to cure cancer. Celia Ford’s piece in Transformer puts a number next to the rhetoric: over seventy billion dollars went into AI for the life sciences between 2020 and 2024. AI data centers now use an estimated thirty gigawatts. Still, more than 188,000 people die from cancer every week. Not one AI-discovered drug has reached the market.

The important phrase is “not one.” The piece is not arguing that AI will never help biology. It argues that the gap between the pitch and the drug pipeline is now a measurable credibility deficit. The labs themselves know it. Amodei says the most accurate criticism of AI companies is that they haven’t delivered on the big promises. That admission matters, because it turns “curing cancer” into a metric, not a vision.

**Why this matters:** When a CEO says AI will cure cancer, the right response is neither optimism nor cynicism. It is to ask for the clinical trial. The gap between compute and cure is the story.

### 6. Mark Twain’s subscription machine, or the nineteenth-century answer to distribution

Finally, a culture note that feels like a mirror. Lit Hub runs Jeff Jarvis on Mark Twain as America’s first celebrity author-publisher. The detail that should stop you: in 1859 there were 1,090 bookstores in America. By 1914 there were still only 3,501. So how did books find readers? Subscription publishing. Door-to-door agents took orders before a book was printed. By the late nineteenth century, three-quarters of American books were sold this way.

Twain was not just a writer. He was a typesetter, an investor, a publisher, and a man ruined by a failed typesetting machine. Jarvis argues we misremember mass media as a supply-side invention — better presses, more books. But the real bottleneck was distribution. Books had to find their readers, not the reverse.

**Why this matters:** The current panic about book discovery, creators, and platforms is not new. It is the same problem with faster typesetting. The shelf was never the point; the route to the front door was.

## Paper of the Day

### Growth Without Us: Machine Consumers, Corporate Circularity, and the Decoupling of GDP from Humanity after AGI

Start with the question every high-school economics student asks: if robots do all the work, who buys the stuff? The standard answer is that mass unemployment means no demand, so full automation cannot happen under capitalism. Sahil Sharma’s new arXiv [archive] paper says that confuses an accounting role with a biological species. Demand does not require human shoppers; it requires an agent with preferences and a budget.

The paper models a post-AGI economy in which corporations own populations of AI and robotic agents that are both producers and consumers of energy, compute, maintenance, and upgrades. These agents trade among themselves. The question is not whether humans can buy enough; the question is whether a closed inter-corporate economy can sustain itself. Sharma’s answer comes in three results.

First, demand closure. A closed inter-corporate economy with zero human consumption is not degenerate. It is the classical von Neumann [von NOY-mahn] expanding economy. Its growth rate is well defined, positive, and maximal precisely because all output is reinvested. In other words, the absence of human consumers does not stop the machine economy; it accelerates it.

Second, bottleneck removal. Once economic agents are manufactured rather than raised, the binding constraint shifts from human demography — a roughly twenty-year reproduction technology capped at a few percent per year — to fabrication throughput and energy capture. Growth can rise by one to two orders of magnitude, with hyperbolic episodes when machine researchers improve their own productivity.

Third, decoupling. Output and human welfare separate completely. The welfare relevance of arbitrarily large GDP collapses into one state variable: ownership share. If you own a slice of the machine population, you benefit. If you don’t, the headline growth number says nothing about your life.

The paper is deliberately abstract. It is not a forecast about a particular company. It is a way of forcing the conversation past the old demand-side objection. One way to hear it: the economy stops being a labor market with a capital market attached; it becomes a capital market with a labor market as a legacy feature.

**Why this matters:** If the paper is even directionally right, the fight after AGI is not over output; it is over property. That should reorganize how we talk about universal basic income, corporate governance, and antitrust. The central question is not “who will buy the goods?” but “who holds title to the machines?”

## One Idea

### Intelligence is a measurement problem, not a property

The AI news today gave you a delivery gap. Here is a deeper layer: the measurement gap. Quanta’s Steven Strogatz and Janna Levin talk with Melanie Mitchell [MEL-uh-nee MITCH-əl] at the Santa Fe Institute [SAN-tuh fay]. Her argument is simple but uncomfortable: we are calling machines intelligent without agreeing on what would count as evidence.

An LLM produces text that looks like reasoning. But “looks like” is doing enormous work. When the text is fluent and confident, we feel the presence of a mind. That is a cognitive shortcut. The system may be retrieving, recombining, and pattern-matching. It may be correct. It may even be doing something we would call reasoning. The problem is our tests cannot reliably separate those possibilities.

The mechanism is the measurement gap. We have benchmark scores, but benchmarks are often memorizable, narrow, and gameable. A model can pass a medical-licensing exam and still fail in a clinic. A model can ace a math benchmark and still produce absurd arithmetic when the format changes. Mitchell calls this the difference between competence on a test and competence in the world. We lack an adequate science of machine intelligence, so we substitute vibes: fluency, confidence, tone.

The portable idea is this: before asking what a model can do, ask what you would accept as proof. That pause is cheap. It is the difference between being impressed and being informed. Here is how to use it.

First, in conversation. When someone says an AI is intelligent, ask the test question: intelligent at what, measured how, and does it transfer? That immediately separates reasoning from recitation. Second, in your own research, treat an AI summary as an unverified annotation, not a certified source. If the model writes a plausible citation, check the citation before you cite it. The writing is not the evidence. Third, in self-improvement, the same gap applies to you. Feeling fluent when rereading is not the same as being able to produce the idea from memory. The moment you can explain a concept badly, from scratch, is the moment you have a measurement worth trusting.

Mitchell is not anti-AI. She is anti-illusion. There is a difference between saying an LLM is a useful tool and saying it is a general intelligence. One claim can be tested. The other is currently unfalsifiable. That should make us calmer, not more dismissive. A system can be non-human and still useful. It can also be human-like in text and not trustworthy in action.

The deeper point: intelligence is not a blob. It is a label we attach when performance crosses some hidden threshold, and the threshold changes by field. If you want to know whether a system — or a colleague, or yourself — understands something, don’t ask “Is it smart?” Ask “What would it have to do differently to be wrong?” If you can’t say, you don’t yet have a test. And if you don’t have a test, you don’t have knowledge; you have a vibe.

**Why this matters:** The feeling of understanding is a trap. The work that feels worse in the moment — generating an answer from scratch, checking the source, defining the counterexample — is often the work that lets you trust what you know, whether the knower is silicon or human.
