## The World

### 1. The price of intelligence is falling faster than almost anything in history

Alex Tabarrok at Marginal Revolution points to a new Epoch AI report with a number that should reframe how you think about AI competition. Over the past three years, the cost of a given level of AI performance has fallen by an average of 47 percent per quarter. That is a thirteen-fold drop every year. The report argues this is faster than any other transformative technology in history.

The concrete example is stark. OpenAI’s o3 cost about thirty cents per question to hit 75 percent on a benchmark called GPQA Diamond in January 2025. By mid-2026, a model called GPT-5.6 Luna reached roughly the same score for four ten-thousandths of a cent. That is a 725-fold decline in under eighteen months.

The argument is not merely that models are getting smarter. It is that a fixed amount of intelligence is becoming dramatically cheaper to run. That changes the open-model debate. An open model can look dangerous because it gives many people a capable system. But if frontier models are also becoming cheap at any given level of performance, the advantage of running an open copy erodes faster than people assume. You are not competing against a fixed expensive model. You are competing against a price curve that falls every quarter.

**Why this matters:** The real moat is no longer model quality alone; it is the rate at which inference cost collapses, and that rate is quietly redistributing who can deploy intelligence profitably.

### 2. Xi Jinping in Washington, and a K-shaped relationship

Xi Jinping [shee jin-ping] is in Washington for his first White House visit since 2015. ChinaTalk’s Jordan Schneider talks with Julian Gewirtz [guh-WIRTS], a former Biden National Security Council China director. The striking point is that there is very little substantive agenda on the table.

So why hold a state visit at all? Gewirtz says the United States is trading symbolism and pageantry for something else: a managed relationship, not a thaw. He describes the US-China relationship as K-shaped. Leader-level diplomacy pulls upward, while economic and security competition pulls downward. The visit is not evidence that the competition is ending. It is a tool for keeping the two tracks from collapsing into a single downward slope.

There is also a flip in the script. Concessions that were once framed as American gifts to China are now treated as concessions from Beijing. And Gewirtz offers a warning: Beijing’s public statements about AI risk do not necessarily tell you how China will handle pacing and safety. Finally, the conversation touches on a succession problem. What the decline of Xi Jinping means for a political system with no clean answer is one of the hardest questions in Chinese politics.

**Why this matters:** The pageantry is not the story; the question is whether leader-level diplomacy can keep the economic and military competition from spiraling while Beijing manages its own internal transition.

### 3. Beijing floats a BRICS zone for open AI

German Euronews, picked up through GDELT machine-translated sources, reports that Xi Jinping has proposed a BRICS [bricks] zone for open AI. This is not a small diplomatic footnote. It is an attempt to build a governance zone for artificial intelligence outside the US-EU axis.

The word “open” matters enormously. Beijing is positioning itself as the champion of open models for the global south, at the exact moment Washington obsesses over closed frontier labs and export controls. The local framing is not about safety grants or Pentagon briefings. It is about who gets to train the next wave of models, and whose rules apply when those models are deployed.

If a BRICS AI zone consolidates, it could split artificial intelligence governance along bloc lines. One set of standards for Washington and Brussels. Another for Brasília, Johannesburg, Moscow, New Delhi, and Beijing. That would affect data flows, liability rules, model release norms, and the open-weight question. It is industrial policy in the language of openness.

**Why this matters:** This is not an AI ethics story; it is an alignment story about which standards and supply chains the next wave of models will follow.

### 4. Machine realism is not neutral sight

A sharp essay in Public Books argues that machine vision is never just a measurement. Morgan Barry’s “The Treachery of Machine Realism” opens with a sentence that should stop you: “The bombed elementary school in Iran appears to have become a target thanks to satellite imaging.”

The point is not that the satellite image was technically wrong. The point is that what the image represented, and to whom, was a matter of life and death. Machine vision systems, including AI-augmented surveillance, produce a realism that feels objective. But behind every image there is an interpretation pipeline: a decision about what counts as a target, what gets flagged, who reviews it, and who is accountable.

Barry’s argument is that machine realism is a political epistemology, not a camera. When we treat computer vision as neutral sight, we move the site of judgment without noticing we moved it. That matters far beyond the military. It applies to insurance, hiring, medical imaging, border control, anywhere a system says it sees something.

**Why this matters:** The danger is not that machines see badly; it is that their realism launders a set of human choices into the appearance of mechanical fact.

### 5. Fady Joudah’s poetry as witness under genocide

Literary Hub profiles Fady Joudah [FAH-dee joo-DAH], a Palestinian physician and poet from Gaza based in Houston. His new multimedia poetry collection, Exhibit G, is assembled in the diaspora but, as the reviewer puts it, never anywhere but Gaza.

The book moves across poems, essays, photographs, and drawings. It is a collaboration among Joudah, Palestinian poets writing from and about Gaza, the Gaza Soup Kitchen, and Gazan photographers documenting the kitchen’s work. The argument is about language and time under genocide.

In stable life, language and time feel like background structures. Under genocide, they are stripped of stability. Silence becomes a refusal of certain speech. The “Exhibit” is the utterance that follows that refusal. The collection is not merely mourning. It is a record of people feeding, burying, and naming themselves while military force continues. That gives poetry a different function: not aesthetic consolation, but infrastructure for survival and witness.

**Why this matters:** The review reframes poetry as a live act of collective memory and daily survival, not a distant response to events.

## Paper of the Day

### Type-safe is not error-free

Today’s paper starts with a simple question: if an AI model is forced to answer using only approved options, does that mean it actually understands what the options mean?

The paper, from Yu Sun and Junhao Xu, is on arXiv [archive]. It is called “Type-Safe Is Not Error-Free.” They study typed decision models, the kind built for software pipelines. Instead of generating free-form text, these models return a decision over a predefined set of options, so the output always fits the schema. That type-safety sounds safe. The authors wondered whether it tells us anything about semantic grounding.

So they ran a clean but disturbing experiment. They took an open-weight model called Jev [jev] and two similar models. Each option has two parts: a short name, and a longer rubric that defines what it means. They changed only the assignment: which name gets attached to which rubric. The question, the state, the rubric wording, and the set of option names all stayed exactly the same.

On 1,200 workflow decisions, renaming two options from zero and one to “no” and “yes” changed 70.4 more answers per hundred. The area under the curve fell from .94 to .23. That is not mild confusion. That is a systematic reversal in the decision ranking. The model was not unsure; it was confidently reading the label rather than the definition.

With neutral option names, the same change did almost nothing. The effect held across all four predicates, and was over seven times larger than under the neutral control. The lesson is sharp: schema conformance is not semantic fidelity. If your software consumes a model’s options, and someone changes a label in a config file, you can silently flip decisions.

That should worry anyone deploying typed model outputs in agents, evaluation harnesses, or safety-critical workflows. It also connects to a bigger debate about benchmarks. Multiple-choice tests may be scoring surface lexical patterns, not understanding. If your eval options shift from “A/B” to “positive/negative,” the scores may not mean what you think.

**Why this matters:** Type safety guarantees the shape of the answer, but not the meaning of the answer — and that gap is exactly where silent failures live.

## One Idea

### The math transfers, even when the physics does not

This morning I want to sit with two ideas, because both are about transfer: when a tool from one domain gets borrowed by another, what exactly is moving?

First, from Quanta Magazine. The headline says biology might not be quantum, but its math is quantumlike. Two decades ago, scientists seemed on the verge of understanding biology in a new, quantum way. That mostly stalled. Life at the molecular level does not really reach the quantum realm where particles act like waves. But the mathematics built for quantum systems — the formalism for interference, uncertainty, and open systems — turns out to be useful for biology.

The point is subtle. This is not a claim that molecules are quantum computers. It is a claim about mathematical homology. The same equations describe different physical systems because the structure of the problem is similar. In your own work, that means you should distinguish between a physical analogy and a formal one. A physical analogy says: the brain is like a computer. A formal one says: the equations that describe phase transitions also describe opinion cascades. The first is a metaphor. The second is a mechanism.

When someone says quantum biology, you can now respond with a more useful sentence: the physics may not transfer, but the math does, and that transfer is the interesting part. That protects you from both woo and reflex skepticism. In conversation, it lets you ask the right question: what mathematical object is being borrowed, not what physical substance is being claimed.

**Why this matters:** The distinction between a physical analogy and a formal homology is what separates a useful model from a fashionable language game.

Second, from Works in Progress, an essay called “Weapons of mass decentralization.” The historical claim is that iron weaponry brought down the Bronze Age empires. Bronze was expensive and required long supply chains for tin and copper. Chariots, and the aristocrats who fought from them, were expensive too. Iron ore was widespread and cheap, so it democratized lethal force. New classes of fighters could challenge palace elites and their trade networks.

The essay asks whether drones and cheap precision weapons could do the same today: decentralizing military capacity away from states. A cheap drone does not require a defense ministry. That does not mean states disappear. It means the cost curve of coercion bends toward smaller actors.

The generalizable idea is to ask of any technology whether it raises or lowers the barriers to coordination and coercion. Technologies that lower the cost of violence or information tend to fragment power. Technologies that raise coordination costs for challengers tend to consolidate it. You can apply that lens not just to drones but to AI, encryption, and social platforms.

**Why this matters:** When you see a new tool, do not ask only what it does. Ask who it empowers relative to the incumbent center — that is a sharper question than whether it is good or bad.
