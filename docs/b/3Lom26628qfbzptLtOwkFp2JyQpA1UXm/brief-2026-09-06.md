## The World

### 1. Central banks are quietly pulling gold out of New York

The headline question is direct: Is the U.S. losing its safe-haven status? Behind it is a slower, concrete shift. Central banks — not just Beijing — have been moving gold reserves out of the Federal Reserve’s vaults in New York and bringing them home.

The Morningstar report frames this as a question about dollar hegemony, but the action itself is less dramatic than the phrase “losing safe-haven status” suggests. It is a hedge, not a break. A central bank that keeps gold in New York is making a bet that the U.S. financial system will remain open, predictable, and non-punitive. Repatriation is the opposite bet: that in a crisis, assets held in another country are a liability, not a reserve.

The trigger is not only sanctions on Russia. It is the broader realization that custody and jurisdiction are the same thing. If your gold sits in a vault in Manhattan, you do not fully control it. This is a slow unwinding of a post-war convenience, and it is happening without a single dramatic announcement.

**Why this matters:** The map of monetary trust is being redrawn one shipment at a time, and that shift will show up in gold prices, reserve composition, and the cost of U.S. borrowing long before it shows up in any official statement.

### 2. The new strategic chokepoints are under the ocean

A report from tele.net.in argues that AI, geopolitics, and renewables are all converging on the same forgotten infrastructure: subsea cables. These cables already carry nearly all intercontinental data. AI makes them more critical, because training and inference across distributed data centers need low-latency, high-capacity routes that satellites cannot match.

Renewables are changing where cable landing stations make sense, since cheap power is increasingly coastal and remote. Geopolitics is doing the rest. Cable consortia, repair ships, and landing rights are now treated as security questions, not just commercial contracts.

The report reframes the cables as “critical assets,” not neutral plumbing. That is the argument worth repeating. A cable is not just a wire. It is a jurisdiction, a repair window, a set of permits, and a point of leverage. When a state or company controls the landing station, it controls not the fiber itself but the legal and physical gate around it. The cable may be privately owned, but the security problem is public.

**Why this matters:** If you want a map of the next decade’s pressure points, follow the cable routes and their landing stations — not the flashier space race.

### 3. Chinese AI circles are calling open models a “united front”

ChinaTalk has translated a post from the Chinese blog FuneralAI, and it offers a different lens on the open-weight debate. The piece is blunt. Chinese models have been farming Opus 5 “like a boss monster” — meaning they are chipping away at the leaderboard — but the basic picture has not changed: OpenAI and Anthropic still hold a large lead.

What is new is the argument about what to do about it. The blog calls open models the “united front” of AI. The idea is not that open weights are a gift to the world, but that they are a strategy. Open models let a wider domestic ecosystem build on a shared foundation. They reduce dependence on U.S. API access. They keep the hardware, talent, and evaluation pipeline pointed inward. And they deny the leading labs a clean monopoly on what “the model” is.

This is not a safety argument. It is a competitive positioning argument. The theory of quick victory and the theory of defeat are both rejected; open models are the long game.

**Why this matters:** It inverts the Western frame: open models are not just a leak to be managed — they are a deliberate geopolitical tool for catching up.

### 4. Safety work may not shift the equilibrium it claims to shift

A LessWrong post from Towards_Keeperhood argues that we need equilibrium analysis to assess AI safety work — especially after recent incidents like the Hugging Face release. The core idea is that there are two equilibria.

One governs how much companies spend on safety for commercial reasons: brand protection, liability avoidance, regulation prevention. Companies will fund safety work until the marginal commercial benefit equals the marginal benefit of investing a dollar in capabilities. If you join that work, your counterfactual impact is small. You have not shifted the equilibrium; you have occupied a seat inside it.

The second equilibrium governs risk awareness and government action. The post argues that more ambitious safety approaches — or policy advocacy that changes what governments treat as urgent — can actually move the line. The Hugging Face incident matters because it publicly demonstrated what model release means, which shifts the second equilibrium.

**Why this matters:** It reframes safety contribution not by effort but by which curve you are pushing.

### 5. Short-form video is not a willpower problem; it is a unit-size problem

Tyler Cowen points to a new paper by Renjie Bao of Princeton on short-form media and self-control. The finding is crisp. People on a short-drama platform — one-minute episodes — watch far more than they intended. Paying users watched 82.1 percent more than their own stated plans.

The structural estimates suggest an average temptation horizon of 11.2 minutes. That sounds short, but it is long relative to a one-minute episode. The design matters because short units repeatedly renew temptation, and the temptation lasts longer than any single unit. So what looks like individual weakness is partly a product of the unit size.

The paper’s counterfactuals are useful: larger decision units, default limits, and forced breaks all improve long-run welfare. The policy implication is not moral.

**Why this matters:** If you regulate or design against overconsumption, the lever is not shame — it is episode length and default stopping cues.

### 6. The billboards of Silicon Valley are future ruins

n+1 has an elegant essay by Adrian Daub about the physical signage of the current AI boom. Driving between Redwood City and Mountain View, you see a billboard that says, “YOUR AI AGENT NEEDS A WORKFLOW ENGINE.” That is a cry of triumphant return, Daub writes — and probably one day a monument to a bubble bursting.

The essay is not just jaundiced. It treats billboards as a form of archaeology. Every hype cycle writes its promises on the landscape, and later the same signs become evidence of the cycle’s excess. The pet-food delivery billboard of 1999 and the crypto billboard of 2021 are now historical artifacts. The AI agent workflow engine billboard is next in line.

Daub’s point is that hype is not invisible. It becomes physical. It buys space along the highway. And then it stays there, long after the money has left.

**Why this matters:** It gives you a test for hype: ask whether today’s sign will read as a joke or a tombstone in five years.

## Paper of the Day

### Clean Engineering, Unstable Measurement: an audit of LLM judges

Today’s paper comes from arXiv [archive]. The title is a mouthful, but the story is simple. Imagine a thermometer that gives you one reading at noon and a different reading at 12:05, for the same cup of water. You would not trust it to measure fever. Now imagine that thermometer is an LLM — a language model — used to score other AIs, rank their answers, and decide which training data is good.

The paper asked: if you send the same request to the same model name twice, do you get the same ranking? The preregistered answer is no. Across 52,988 attempts, same-window repeat rankings agreed at a Spearman [SPEER-man] correlation of 0.400, when the team had required 0.90 to call the instrument reliable. Byte-identical next-day replays agreed at 0.78, when they required 0.99.

The engineering was clean. The execution record was at ceiling. So the noise was not in the code. It was in the judge.

Three mechanisms explain the gap. First, the mapping from labels to meaning biased readouts as strongly as the actual signal. Second, the differences between candidate answers were seven orders of magnitude below the instrument’s own noise floor. Third, byte-identical inputs could return different rankings — the instrument itself was unstable.

The authors did not stop there. They tested whether waiting helped. It did not. They tested four providers. They shared a failure pattern. The result is not that LLM judges are useless. It is that they are not yet measurement instruments. They are stochastic storytellers wearing a lab coat.

The so-what is practical. Benchmarks, leaderboards, and training-data filters that depend on LLM judges may be much noisier than their decimal places suggest. A paper that reports “model A beat model B by three points” may be reporting noise.

**Why this matters:** If the judge is noisy, then leaderboard gains, benchmark scores, and training-data decisions built on that judge are partly lottery tickets.

## One Idea

### The surveillance paradox: when the dashboard becomes the job

There is an old idea in social science: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. The Nautilus piece “Your Boss Is Watching You” gives this a current form. AI-assisted surveillance can now track employees minute by minute, keystroke by keystroke. The question it raises is not only whether surveillance is invasive. It is whether surveillance produces the desired effect at all.

The mechanism is worth slowing down. Watch someone and you change the thing you are watching. A person who is measured on keystrokes will produce keystrokes; they may not produce better decisions. A person who is measured on response time will answer fast and maybe avoid the hard message. Autonomy is not a soft benefit; it is part of how people convert effort into judgment. Remove it, and you get a workforce that looks productive on the dashboard while the real work quietly degrades.

This is Goodhart’s law [GOOD-hart] with a camera attached. There is a second mechanism: measurement makes the worker expend energy on the measurement itself. Every minute spent signaling productivity is a minute not spent thinking. The surveillance system becomes a second job. The paradox is that the more precisely you measure output, the more you may reduce the output you actually wanted.

The same logic applies outside the workplace. If you track your reading speed, you may start skimming. If you track your steps, you may choose the easy flat route. If you track your model’s benchmark score, you may optimize for benchmark contamination. The principle is portable: when you introduce a metric, ask what behavior the metric is about to create. Then ask whether that behavior is the one you want. If not, do not add more measurement — add more trust.

In conversation, this is a useful filter. When a friend says they are being tracked at work, don’t just ask whether the tracking is fair. Ask what the tracking is selecting for. The most dangerous metric is the one that is easy to collect but orthogonal to the real goal. In your own research, the equivalent is the convenience metric: the loss curve, the survey response, the leaderboard rank. The paper earlier about LLM judges is a case in point — a metric that looks objective but fails reliability.

In self-improvement, the trap is the app that measures streaks. Streaks select for not breaking the chain, which may not select for learning. A slightly harder retrieval session feels worse than rereading, but it is the work that sticks.

**Why this matters:** If you design an evaluation, a management system, or a self-improvement habit, the act of measuring changes the system — so choose metrics that create the behavior you want, not the illusion of control.
