## The World

### 1. Iran diplomacy, but the real story is China and the Strait of Hormuz [hor-MOOZ]

Tehran is making diplomatic noises toward Washington, and China is pushing to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. The wire lede goes to the diplomacy. The English-language press in New Delhi goes to the oil. Their frame: this is not a thaw, it is a supply-chain emergency. If Hormuz remains tight, Indian refiners face longer voyages, higher insurance rates, and a crude premium that hits consumer prices within weeks. China, which imports more crude than anyone through those waters, needs the strait open not because it wants to play mediator, but because it cannot afford a shut lane. The Indian coverage keeps returning to who pays the freight, who reroutes, and who gets forced into expensive alternatives. That tells you the real order of leverage: the importer with the largest tanker queue has the most urgent reason to lean on Tehran. Washington may want a nuclear deal. Beijing wants the water open. Those are different negotiations, and they will not end on the same timetable.  

**Why this matters:** It changes who you watch—if you are only watching Washington and Tehran, you will miss the pressure coming from Beijing and New Delhi.

### 2. The killing of Palestinian scholars as an attack on the archive

Public Books has published a memorial, not just an essay. It names more than 200 Palestinian academic staff killed since the genocide began, many in targeted attacks. It reports that 87,000 students have suffered a near-total disruption of their studies. This is usually covered as a body count, or as a campus story, or not covered at all. The Public Books piece does something different: it treats the deaths of professors, researchers, poets, translators, and librarians as the destruction of intellectual infrastructure. A university is not only buildings. It is the people who remember how to teach, how to translate, how to preserve archives, how to argue with a tradition. When those people are killed, the loss does not show up in the daily casualty figures. It shows up twenty years later, when a field has no one left to train the next generation. The piece asks readers to remember them not as statistics but as scholars with actual projects, actual students, actual unfinished work. That is harder to consume, because it demands a slower kind of attention.  

**Why this matters:** If you only count the dead, you miss the war on memory itself.

### 3. The AI-risk “regulatory capture” story is a lazy cynicism

Alex Tabarrok [TAB-uh-rok] has a sharp post titled “What Regulatory Capture Actually Looks Like.” His target is the idea that AI executives only warn about AI risk because regulation would entrench them. He says the heuristic “people believe what serves their interests” is useful, but it has taken over their brains. Dario Amodei [DAH-ree-oh ah-MOH-day] saying AI might be dangerous is not a clever marketing pitch. “Our product might kill you” is a terrible sales line. Amodei, Sam Altman, and Elon Musk were publicly warning about AI risk before they had AI companies to promote. Nobel laureate Geoffrey Hinton [HIN-tun] left a high-paying job at Google precisely so he could speak out. The worry is not confined to CEOs. Rank-and-file researchers share it, and so do computer scientists with nothing to sell. So the regulatory-capture story fails as a general explanation. That does not mean the AI safety movement is above criticism. It means the lazy version—calling every warning self-interest—does not tell you what to regulate, how fast, or by whom. It just flatters the cynic.  

**Why this matters:** If you misread sincere risk as a sales pitch, you will misdiagnose the actual policy problem.

### 4. An Egyptian prison memoir pushes back against English legibility

Literary Hub has an essay by Abdelrahman ElGendy [AB-del-rah-mahn el-GEN-dee] about his memoir Huna [HOO-nah]. Huna is a memoir of the Egyptian revolution, six years of political imprisonment, and exile in the United States. ElGendy writes it in English, and he says that means writing inside a contradiction. The Arab prison memoir in English often arrives already framed as a torture spectacle: a detached reader can consume suffering as evidence of the Other’s barbarity, or as an exercise in goodwill empathy with the unfortunate Other “over there.” The Palestinian poet Fady Joudah [FAH-dee JOO-dah] calls this a “parody of the self”—the self made to perform a recognizable, palatable version of its wound in order to be legible. ElGendy says that tension sharpened during the Gaza genocide, because English was the language used to euphemize and authorize the killing. He structured his memoir around an Egyptian prison poem. That choice is the argument: poetry does not translate as smoothly as testimony, and the parts that resist translation may be the most honest parts.  

**Why this matters:** It changes the question you ask of any conflict memoir—not “is it authentic?” but “what did the language force the author to perform?”

### 5. The internet’s “vibe” aesthetic mistakes the look of the sublime for the thing itself

The Point magazine has an essay called “After the Sublime,” by Jonathan Kay. His claim: online “vibe” content—Backrooms videos, liminal spaces, the endless empty mall—borrows the surface of the sublime without understanding it. The eighteenth-century sublime was not just a mood. It was an encounter with something so large or vast that reason fails, and then reason comes back with a humbling sense of its own limits. The Backrooms genre copies the visual grammar: empty space, low hum, absence. But it flattens that into an aesthetic vibe, a background mood you can scroll through. There is no failure of understanding; there is only recognition of a style. Kay says the problem is not that the internet uses vibe; it is that it mistakes vibe for the thing vibe points at. That explains why so much algorithmic culture feels familiar and empty at the same time. It is the difference between silence and muting.  

**Why this matters:** It gives you a precise phrase for what culture is being optimized for—recognition, not encounter.

## Paper of the Day

### Decomposition Buys Integrity, Not Yield

Here is a paper that asks a simple question: if you split a big research task across a tree of AI agents, do you actually get more findings?  

Rong He [RONG HUH]’s paper, “Decomposition Buys Integrity, Not Yield,” says no—or at least not in the way most people assume. Imagine a task with a hundred relevant findings. You split it into ten agents. Each agent reads ten items and passes up the one it thinks matters most. At the next level, another agent reads those ten summaries and passes up one. The pipeline looks cleaner, more parallel, more professional. But the arithmetic is cruel. If each agent keeps one item out of b with probability one-over-b—perfect filtering—then every tree shape delivers exactly one finding to the top. A deep tree and a flat one end at the same place. The decomposition gave you integrity: smaller contexts, cleaner separation. It did not give you more findings.  

The paper goes further. When retention follows a power law—when an agent handed b items keeps one with probability C times b to the minus delta—the total findings that reach the root is C to the k times N to the one-minus-delta. The task size and the architecture separate. The architecture contributes only C per level, and C is at most one. So flat is optimal for yield. No clever arrangement of agents escapes the exponent.  

Then He tests it. On six hundred production deep-research traces, delta is 0.34. That means a hundred findings become about four and a half. At a hop where item boundaries come from the tool rather than a text heuristic, C is 0.571, observed across sixteen thousand hops—not extrapolated. A single item has only about a 57 percent chance of surviving one handoff. And alignment costs something too: one brief in sixteen goes off-target.  

The so-what is not that multi-agent systems are useless. They are useful when you need clean separation, parallel work, or audit trails. But they do not buy yield. The findings die at the handoffs. If you are designing a research pipeline, the question is not “how many agents?” but “what fraction survives each hop?” And if you are a human in an organization, the same logic applies: every delegation layer is a filter. The deeper the tree, the more quietly the good stuff disappears.  

**Why this matters:** The next time someone says a multi-agent pipeline will “read more,” ask what fraction of findings survives each handoff.

## One Idea

### Desirable difficulties, applied

Here is an idea that sounds wrong: the more fluent studying feels, the less you may be learning.  

The psychologist Robert Bjork [BYORK] called the mechanism desirable difficulties. Retrieval, spacing, interleaving, generation—each one adds friction. That friction feels worse than rereading, but it produces stronger, more flexible memory. Rereading feels smooth because the page is doing the work. The moment you close the page, you discover you were only recognizing the ideas, not producing them. That gap between recognition and recall is exactly where learning happens, and most people skip it because it feels like failure.  

So how do you use it on a normal Wednesday?  

In conversation: if you just read something sharp, do not paste a link and move on. Close the tab, wait thirty seconds, and say the argument aloud as if the author were not there. Start with the conclusion. The place you stall is your real understanding gap. Then, when you explain it to someone else, you are doing a second retrieval. That is why people who explain things tend to remember them—not because they are smarter, but because they rehearsed production.  

In your own research: before you open the next paper or notebook, write the question from memory. Then write the best answer you currently have—one page, no peeking. The parts you skip because you assume you know them are the parts that will break under pressure. If you are reading a paper, write the abstract in your own words before you copy the title. Then compare. The mismatch is not an indictment; it is a revision list.  

In high-stakes communication: Camille B.’s notes from teaching technical people to speak in public open with the same idea in a different form: deliberate practice is the bottleneck. The target audience is analytic, sometimes shy, and suddenly being asked about AI safety or superintelligence. Her rules are practical. Speak like you write—fluid, uninterrupted sentences. Open with spoilers. Make your voice go higher and lower than usual. Be aware of the social context. But the deeper point is why those rules work: they create desirable difficulty. Producing a full, honest sentence under time pressure is harder than pointing to a slide. Recording yourself and listening back is uncomfortable because it reveals the gaps. That discomfort is the sign the practice is working.  

A simple calibration: after you finish a chapter or a talk, write down three questions you would ask a student. Then answer them without notes. If you can answer them fluently, the difficulty was too low; space the session out further, or interleave it with another topic. The goal is not misery; it is the minimum effective friction. For interviews, do not just prepare key messages—record a two-minute answer to the hardest question you expect, then transcribe the first thirty seconds. The places where the grammar falls apart are the places you did not yet know what you thought.  

The trap is to mistake ease for mastery. If a study session, a conversation, or a rehearsal never makes you feel slightly stupid, it was probably too easy. The work that feels worse in the moment is often the work that sticks.
