## The World

### 1. Stolen pickups, Middle Eastern ports, and Russia’s sanctions black market

Via Marginal Revolution and a New York Times investigation: Western export bans have not reduced Russian demand for brand-name trucks. They have moved that demand into a criminal supply chain. The cars in question are not obscure. Dodge Rams, Toyota Tundras, Ford F-150s. Stolen in Canada, hidden in shipping containers, routed through Middle Eastern ports, and sold in Russia. Interpol [IN-ter-pohl] data obtained by the Times shows 4,798 stolen Canadian vehicles were found in Russia between February 2024 and July 2026. That figure, law enforcement officials say, significantly undercounts the problem. But Russia is by far the top international destination for Canadian cars reported to Interpol, according to the Royal Canadian Mounted Police.

The economic story here is not just theft. It is an example of sanctions as market design. When you cut off legal channels, you do not eliminate demand. You hand that demand to actors who do not care about insurance premiums, customs paperwork, or export controls. The result is a sophisticated network of criminals who steal cars, hide them, ship them, and sell them at a premium. In other words, sanctions created a new arbitrage opportunity.

**Why this matters:** Sanctions are usually debated as pressure on a regime, but they are also market rules — and the black market is the unregulated counterpart that fills the gap.

### 2. Iran’s nuclear debate has moved from diplomacy to deterrence

A new analysis from Clingendael [KLING-en-dahl], the Dutch foreign-affairs institute, makes a sharp argument: war has rewritten Iran’s nuclear debate. Before the latest regional escalation, the dominant Iranian framing treated enrichment as a bargaining chip — something to trade away for sanctions relief. Now, according to the analysis, the conversation among Iranian security elites has shifted. The question is no longer how much enrichment do we need for leverage. The question is what level of capability deters an attack.

That is a different logic. Deterrence logic justifies weaponization as self-insurance, not as negotiation. It does not require a test, a weapon, or even a formal declaration. It only requires a credible threshold. Once that framing hardens inside Tehran, the old nuclear-diplomacy playbook — sanctions, inspections, snap-back provisions — loses purchase. You cannot negotiate with a country that has stopped treating enrichment as a price signal and started treating it as survival.

**Why this matters:** A debate shift inside one country can kill an entire nonproliferation architecture, even if no bomb is tested and no treaty is formally broken.

### 3. Taiwan’s Morris Chang, the death of globalization, and a three-year lag

A Chinese-language report from Newtalk, picked up by GDELT [GEE-delt], revisits a prediction from TSMC founder Morris Chang [MAW-ris CHAHNG] that globalization is dead. Analysts like Li Chun [lee CHUN] argue the prediction has now come true — but with a twist. The real supply-chain dispersion benefits will not appear overnight. Li points to a three-year lag. Companies are still paying the duplication costs: building fabs in Arizona, Japan, Germany. But by roughly 2029, the benefits of distributed manufacturing should start to show.

The local framing matters. Western coverage tends to treat reshoring as either a national-security win or a cost problem. Taiwanese commentary is cooler. It treats fragmentation as a structural change in who can produce advanced chips — and it is already thinking about Taiwan’s role once the island is no longer the single choke point. The phrase used in the piece is not alarmist; it is almost managerial. That is the signal. The people closest to the old supply chain are now planning for its dissipation.

**Why this matters:** Supply chains are not just moving. The timetable for who benefits is being set now, and it is not the same timetable Washington uses.

### 4. Dresden was not innocent: the Vonnegut myth under pressure

Literary Hub runs an essay by Susan Farrell on the fact and fiction of Kurt Vonnegut [VON-uh-gut]’s Dresden. In _Slaughterhouse-Five_, Billy Pilgrim sees Dresden as an untouched jewel, a city that had “not suffered so much as a cracked windowpane.” Farrell argues that image is a myth. Dresden was beautiful, yes. The opera house was open. Cinema was running. But it was not innocent. Farrell writes that everyday Dresdeners had witnessed, and often eagerly participated in, the fierce anti-Semitism that infected the country. She cites the grim statistic that 90 percent of Dresden’s Jewish-Polish population had been deported or killed by the time of the bombing.

This reframing changes the moral arithmetic of the novel. Vonnegut used Dresden’s alleged innocence to indict the Allied bombing campaign. If that innocence is overstated, the novel’s pacifist argument becomes less comfortable. It does not erase the horror of the firebombing. But it removes the clean moral contrast. The city was a cultural jewel and part of a genocidal state. Both things were true.

**Why this matters:** A literary myth can shape how a generation remembers a war — and the corrective is messier than the syllabus.

## Paper of the Day

### Spacing effects, revisited for the AI-tutoring era

A new replication in the learning-science world asks a simple question: does the famous spacing effect survive when the tutor is an app, not a lab assistant?

The original finding came from Cepeda [seh-PEH-dah] and colleagues. Memory retention depends not just on how much you study, but on how the gaps are spaced. A gap that is too short feels good but does not pay off. A gap that is too long lets the memory decay. The optimal gap scales with how long you want to remember. For a test in one week, review after one or two days. For a final exam in six months, you want gaps of three to five weeks. The rough rule of thumb is ten to twenty percent of the desired retention interval.

The story in this new replication is not that the lab results were wrong. It is that app-based review changes who controls the spacing. When an AI tutor set the schedule, the spacing effect replicated strongly. Students in the experimental condition retained about thirty-two percent more material after eight weeks than a massed-review control group. But when learners were allowed to choose their own review timing, the effect mostly disappeared.

Why? People selected shorter gaps than the algorithm recommended. They reviewed the next day, then the next, because that felt productive. They confused fluency with retention. The app made the work feel easier, and the learners chased that ease. The algorithm, by contrast, inserted desirable difficulty.

The so-what is directly usable. If you are using an AI tutor, do not override its schedule toward tighter spacing. If you are building one, do not ask the user whether she wants to review again today. Ask whether she wants to remember this in a year. The gap should scale with the desired retention interval, not with the user’s feeling of ease.

**Why this matters:** The optimal gap is not folklore. It is a measurable design parameter, and the fight in AI tutoring is over who sets it.

## One Idea

### Clever Hans and the cues you don’t know you’re giving

Early 1900s Berlin. A retired schoolteacher named Wilhelm von Osten [VIL-helm fon OSS-ten] taught a horse, Clever Hans, to answer questions. Not just arithmetic. Square roots, reading, telling time, identifying musical intervals. Hans would tap his hoof to give the answer. Crowds gathered. Scientists were baffled. A commission investigated and found no evidence of fraud. But a young psychologist named Oskar Pfungst [FOONKST] was not satisfied. He ran the right experiment.

Pfungst had the questioner stand where the horse could not see him. Or he had someone ask a question whose answer the questioner did not know. When the human did not know the answer, Hans failed. When the human knew it, Hans could answer even if the question was whispered from another room.

Pfungst’s conclusion was uncomfortable. Hans was not reading minds. He was reading the human. After every correct tap, the questioner relaxed his shoulders, lifted his head a fraction, breathed differently. The horse had learned to stop tapping at that cue. The human was leaking the answer through expectancy. Pfungst called it the Clever Hans effect, and later researchers renamed it the observer-expectancy effect.

It is not about horses. It is about how every interaction between a learner and a teacher, a subject and a researcher, a staffer and a boss is saturated with tiny signals about what the expected answer is. Why this matters: the feeling of being understood can be an illusion produced by your own cues. A therapist, a tutor, a colleague, a large language model — they can all pick up on your nods, pauses, and leading questions, and give you back what you wanted. That is not insight. That is a mirror.

How to use it. In conversation, stop nodding when someone says what you hope. Ask open questions, wait, and let the silence sit. If you are a manager, do not ask, “Don’t you think the current vendor is too slow?” because the employee will produce Clever Hans answers. Ask, “What is the vendor doing well, and where are the failures?” Then do not fill the pause.

In your own research, if you run surveys, interviews, or models, build in blinding. Pre-register the analysis before seeing data. Randomize the order of conditions. Record interactions so you can check later whether you cued the respondent. The Clever Hans effect has inflated some of the most intuitive findings in psychology, medicine, and animal cognition. If the person scoring the outcome knows the condition, the effect size tends to grow. That is not a niche methods issue. It is a bias that lives in your body.

In self-improvement and study, treat fluency with suspicion. If an AI tutor asks you a question and you hesitate, and it quickly hints, you may have done the equivalent of tapping a hoof until the tutor’s posture told you the answer. The work that feels worse — generating the answer before seeing the hint — is the work that sticks. Desirable difficulties are the antidote to Clever Hans. They force you to produce, not just recognize.

So next time someone seems to understand you perfectly, ask: did they read me, or did I leak the answer? Hans was not a mind reader. He was a cue reader.

**Why this matters:** The feeling of ease in being understood is often a trap made of your own small cues.
