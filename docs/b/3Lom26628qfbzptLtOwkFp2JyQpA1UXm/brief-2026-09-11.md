## The World

### 1. Twenty-five years after the towers, the war that could not be won still needs a better explanation

Today is the twenty-fifth anniversary of 9/11. Most coverage will lead with memorials in New York and Washington. But the best use of your commute may be a ChinaTalk conversation with Wesley Morgan, author of *The Hardest Place*, the Colby Award–winning history of the American military in Afghanistan’s Pech [PESH] Valley and Korengal [KOR-eng-gahl]. Morgan began embedding there when he was a nineteen-year-old freelancer writing for a college paper. His point is simple and brutal: the outposts America built to secure villages ended up drawing the war into them. At Combat Outpost Michigan, at the mouth of the Korengal, a company commander told visiting reporters to expect the first barrage around seven p.m. — two or three a day, like weather. Morgan describes “bubbles of danger,” not clear front lines. And in the middle of the surge, a battalion commander was trying to sell a pullout, because he could see the outpost strategy was generating its own violence. Three separate American wars — conventional infantry, special operations forces, and a JSOC task force — fought disconnected campaigns in the same valleys. Morgan tells the story of two Green Beret teams in 2004: one refused to be used as muscle in a local timber feud, and one went in as hard and as often as it could. The result was not a failure of will, but a failure of institutional sight.

**Why this matters:** The anniversary invites memorial language; this conversation gives you the harder, more useful history — not what we intended, but what the outposts actually produced.

### 2. One resignation turned AI fear into a wildfire

The AI story of the week is not a model release. It is a resignation. Jacob Coxon, a researcher at Anthropic [AN-thruh-pik], left the lab and said it and OpenAI were, quote, “racing straight to self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives.” Then Anthropic’s alignment lead, Evan Hubinger, added that staff really do earnestly believe AI could kill all humans. The post went viral. Nathan Lambert’s essay for Interconnects asks not whether the claim is right, but why this particular resignation reached the mainstream when similar warnings had smoldered inside the AI community for years. His answer is a fire metaphor. For years, people were striking matches about AI risk, but the ground was damp. The sparks would smolder in a small community and burn out, unnoticed. Then the ground dried out. The OpenAI-HuggingFace incident, a dramatic result on the Navier-Stokes equations, and the general rise in AI stakes made outsiders suddenly think they should care. When a credible insider lit a match, it caught. The essay’s sharpest observation is that the public did not latch onto the median safety view. It latched onto a specific wing — the one assigning moderate probabilities to mass extinction. That selection process is not random. It will determine which policies sound reasonable.

**Why this matters:** The framing that reaches voters and members of Congress will shape the next laws, and it is not the only safety frame on offer.

### 3. The youth mental health data nobody expected

A quietly important release from the National Survey on Drug Use and Health landed Monday, and Tyler Cowen flagged it because it cuts against the dominant story. From 2021 to 2025, adolescents aged twelve to seventeen reported lower use of tobacco, alcohol, marijuana, and binge drinking in the previous month. Fewer started drinking alcohol, vaping, or using marijuana in the past year. Fewer reported illicit drug use and substance use disorders. And, more strikingly, fewer reported major depressive episodes, serious thoughts of suicide, making a suicide plan, or attempting suicide. That is a broad trend, not one metric. There is a wrinkle: the entire team responsible for the survey was laid off last year, so the data arrives with uncertainty about future rounds and about whether the numbers will keep moving in the same direction. But the 2025 report exists, and the direction is consistent across multiple indicators. It does not mean the teen mental health crisis was invented. It does mean the story may be more dynamic than a straight line of decline.

**Why this matters:** If the trend is real, it changes what you can confidently say about phones, schools, and adolescent mental health — you may need to update against the crisis narrative.

### 4. The kites that rattled a state

Literary Hub published an essay by Ahmad Ibsais [AH-muhd ib-SAYS] that is less about literature than about what a state chooses to fear. It tells the story of Khaled Al Khateeb and his siblings in Gaza, who spent a week making a kite from scraps they found at home. When it flew, Ibsais writes, it was as if Khaled himself was soaring above the drones and the bombs. Then the essay shifts: Israel’s defense minister said kites would be treated like drones, and Benjamin Netanyahu threatened that people launching them could be killed. Ibsais argues that a colonial power that has destroyed thousands of children now declares itself endangered by paper and string. Everything but paper and string had already been stolen from Khaled and his friends, he writes, yet Israel declared itself endangered. He connects this to the Nakba [NOCK-bah], the displacement of 1948, and to the old colonizer adage that the old will die and the young will forget. The essay’s reframe is uncomfortable and precise: the kite is not a weapon; it is the survival of a child who was supposed to forget. The reaction is to treat memory itself as a threat.

**Why this matters:** This is culture being argued now, and it forces a question about how threat is defined, and by whom, in an asymmetrical war.

**Why this matters:** The next few months will determine whether AI law is written in the heat of a particular scare or with room for the median technical view.

## Paper of the Day

### Spacing effects, revisited for the AI-tutoring era

Let me tell you about a paper that asks a very old question with a very new tool. In 2006, Cepeda [seh-PEH-dah] and colleagues published a famous result: if you want to remember something for a week, you should not practice it every day. You should space your practice, and the optimal gap between sessions scales with how long you want to remember. For a test in a week, leave about a day or two between reviews. For a test in a year, the gap should be around a month. The old studies were done with flashcards and laboratory sessions, where researchers controlled the calendar. The new paper asks: does that rule survive when the learner is on a phone, in a bus, doing five-minute sessions from an app?

The researchers took several thousand adults using an app-based review system. They did not assign fixed gaps from a lab calendar. Instead, they looked at the app’s own scheduling algorithm, then varied it in a randomized trial. One group got the Cepeda-style optimal spacing, with gaps set as a fraction of the desired retention interval. Another group got the fluent, close-together review that apps often default to because it feels better and keeps people engaged. The outcome was not immediate app performance. It was a test sixty days later.

The result was not subtle. The optimally spaced group remembered roughly twice as much after two months as the tight-review group, even though both groups spent the same total time. And the effect was largest for people who had the least prior knowledge. The so-what is directly usable: a gap of roughly one-tenth to one-fifth of the retention interval works even in micro-sessions. If you want to remember something for six months, review it about every three to six weeks, not every day. The app does not need to be fancy. It needs to be quiet long enough for the forgetting to start.

**Why this matters:** The paper gives a simple rule for your own learning — whether you use an app or old paper cards, the calendar, not the effort, is doing the work.

## One Idea

### Architected taste and the myth of pure subjectivity

The first idea comes from a Works in Progress essay with a simple, slightly annoying claim: taste is subjective, yes, but when it comes to architecture, there is a surprising level of agreement. Show people buildings and ask them to rate them, and the pattern is not random. Traditional forms — ornament, human-scaled windows, clear entryways, local materials — consistently outscore the blank glass slabs that architects have championed for a century. The essay is not just aesthetic grumbling. It makes an empirical point: we treat architectural preference as something elites impose or individuals choose, but the data suggest a shared baseline. People know what they like, and they like buildings that read as buildings.

Now apply that as a mental model. When someone tells you a preference is purely subjective, ask: have we measured it? The same move appears in food, music, universities, and AI interfaces. You can say “taste is subjective” to end a conversation, or you can treat it as a hypothesis and look for hidden consensus. In your own research, if you are designing something — a product, a paper, an argument — do not assume your audience’s taste is arbitrary. Test for the shared baseline first. In conversation, when someone says there is no accounting for taste, you can respond that actually, when you ask people directly, the agreement is often stronger than the experts predicted. That is a more interesting conversation.

**Why this matters:** The line “taste is subjective” is often an excuse for not measuring what people actually prefer, and the measurement can surprise you.

### Fossil spirals and the search for hidden drivers

The second idea is more planetary. Quanta Magazine reports on foraminifera [for-uh-min-IF-er-uh], tiny ocean plankton that build spiral shells. They are among the most abundant eukaryotic plankton on Earth, found from the tropics to the high latitudes. For thousands or sometimes millions of years, all over the world, they built their spirals in one direction. Then, suddenly, the spirals flipped direction at the same time, everywhere, only to flip back later. This is not one species drifting. It is a synchronized global switch. The scientists studying it do not yet know the driver. It might be ocean chemistry, temperature, or something else. The puzzle is the point.

Here is the portable version. When you see independent actors change together — companies adopting the same policy, students shifting majors at the same time, voters in different countries turning the same way — do not look only at local incentives. Look for a hidden global forcing. The forams were not coordinating. Something in the shared environment flipped the advantage. The same logic applies to fads, market panics, and cultural shifts. The most useful question is not “why did this actor change?” but “what changed for all of them at once?” If a dozen unrelated labs adopt the same safety language in the same month, do not assume they all read the same memo. Ask what changed in the environment that made that language advantageous. That is how you see the next switch coming.

**Why this matters:** Detecting hidden drivers before your competitors do is a research advantage, and the fossil record is a good place to practice that instinct.
